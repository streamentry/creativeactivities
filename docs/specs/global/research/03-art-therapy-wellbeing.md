# Art therapy, creative wellbeing and responsible healing claims

## 1. Scope and date

- **Date of research:** 2026-11-14. All URLs accessed 2026-11-14 unless noted.
- **Scope:** how professional bodies define and regulate art therapy; the evidence base for arts and wellbeing; arts-on-prescription and museum programs; creative-wellbeing apps and products; trauma-informed and grief-sensitive practice; crisis signposting; and honest wording for a free creative-activities product.
- **Method limits:** Searches returned summaries and abstracts for most sources. Only two pages were fetched directly (AATA "About"; the WHO report page was blocked by a verification wall). Figures below are as reported by the cited source and should be checked against the full text before publication. Search-volume data for queries such as "art therapy activities" was **not** obtained; see section 3 for how to measure it.
- **Governing rule (from MISSION.md and DESIGN.md):** we describe activities as creative, recreational, supportive or engaging. We never call an activity therapy, treatment or healing unless a qualified professional delivers or reviews it.

## 3. Feature patterns worth adopting

1. **Non-clinical framing with an explicit scope line.** Store listings with an explicit "not a medical device" line are common for health-adjacent apps; the Google Play health-app policy requires that statement for non-device health apps and bars false or misleading health claims ([Google Play policy, as cited in docs/plan/competitors.md](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12261419)). Put the same sentence on every activity page: "This is a creative activity, not therapy or medical advice."
2. **Mood-linked palettes and reflection, without scoring.** Lake's palette-from-feeling idea is worth testing as a "choose a color for how you feel today" prompt. Do not turn it into a mood score or a streak.
3. **Print-first and offline-first delivery.** The Activity Card in DESIGN.md is a stronger distinction than any app feature the reviewed apps offer.
4. **Permission to stop.** Morning Pages and the artist-date model both say the practice has no wrong output. Use the same tone for "It is fine to stop."
5. **Choice of mode.** Offer "make it yourself," "do it alongside someone," and "watch and talk" as equal paths. Several apps are solo-only.

## 4. Content patterns

### 4.1 What people are likely searching for

The brief's example queries ("art therapy activities", "therapeutic art", "art for anxiety") are hypotheses. **No search volume was pulled for this report.** Before writing hubs, pull impression and click data from Google Search Console and a keyword tool, and check `keywords.csv` in the repo for existing demand signals. Expect three intent groups:

- **Learn about the profession** ("what is art therapy", "how to become an art therapist"). Answer briefly, link to AATA, ATCB and BAAT, and do not sell activities here.
- **Self-help for a feeling** ("art for anxiety", "calming art activity"). Answer with creative activities framed as calming or enjoyable, plus crisis and professional signposting.
- **Activities for a person or group** ("art activities for dementia", "craft for grandma with arthritis"). This is the core product fit.

### 4.2 Safe self-guided offers versus professional work

Self-guided creative activities are reasonable when they are presented as recreation and self-expression. Activities that explore trauma narratives, grief in depth, or personal history, or that are framed as addressing a diagnosis, belong with a qualified facilitator. Mark those explicitly and do not publish them as self-guided.

### 4.3 Trauma-informed and grief-sensitive design

- **Principles:** SAMHSA's 2014 concept of trauma and six principles (safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; cultural, historical and gender issues) are the most-cited framework ([SAMHSA SMA 14-4884, via Mass.gov copy](https://www.mass.gov/doc/samhsas-concept-of-trauma-and-guidance-for-a-trauma-informed-approach-2014/download)).
- **Gap:** A scoping review by Sunderland and colleagues found no specific guidelines for trauma-aware practice in arts-health or community arts, and proposed interim principles instead ([search summary; full citation to confirm]). The Association of Arts Education Partners and Ingenuity guidebook on trauma-informed, healing-centred practice through the arts is the most practical practitioner resource found ([AEP](https://aep-arts.org/a-model-for-trauma-informed-and-healing-centered-practice-through-the-arts_session-summary)).
- **Grief:** Weiskittle and Gramling (2018) pooled 27 studies of visual-art approaches to bereavement. They found associations with continued bonds and meaning-making, but efficacy evidence was mixed and not meta-analyzable. Their proposed purposes for art-making (confronting mortality, meaning-making, crisis resolution, authentic expression) are a useful design checklist, not a clinical protocol.
- **Design implications:** Never ask someone to draw their loss, trauma or diagnosis. Keep prompts about materials, shapes and sensory experience by default. Let people choose a blank, a template or "no drawing, just color." Include an always-visible exit ("Stop here").

### 4.4 Crisis-safe design and signposting

- **Trigger:** Any activity that invites personal disclosure, journaling about feelings, or grief should carry a visible, plain-language crisis route. Do not wait for a keyword match.
- **Routes to verify before publishing:** Samaritans, 116 123 (UK and Ireland, free, 24/7; [NHS suicide page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/suicide/)). Shout, text 85258 (UK). 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call, text or chat (US; third-party sources only, so check the official 988 site). Local emergency number.
- **Do not** diagnose, score or label a user from their answers. Do not show a "wellbeing score."
- **Do not** store disclosures in analytics events. DESIGN.md already requires this; the activity name is the event payload, not the story.

### 4.5 Museums, libraries and arts-on-prescription

- **Museums on Prescription (UCL, London and Kent):** 115 socially isolated older adults referred to 10-week museum programmes, measured with the UCL Museum Wellbeing Measure. Reported significant improvement across all wellbeing and loneliness measures ([EHPS study](https://www.ehps.net/ehp/index.php/contents/article/view/2781); [Perspectives in Public Health, 2018, per search summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29130869/)). Pre-post design; I did not confirm a control group.
- **Prescribe Culture (University of Edinburgh):** a small self-reported evaluation; 100% of members agreed participation supported connection. Sample small; funding uncertain.
- **NASP 2022 rapid review** (Mughal, Polley, Sabey and Chatterjee): arts and heritage on-prescription models appear beneficial; gaps remain for under-served audiences ([NASP briefing](https://socialprescribingacademy.org.uk/media/dxslxrxu/nasp-briefing-arts-culture-heritage.pdf)).
- **"Culture on Prescription":** I could not find a Museums Association or Arts Council England program by this name. Do not cite it until verified.

## 5. Evidence and claim wording

Strength ratings are my judgement from the sources above: **Strong** (multiple consistent high-quality reviews), **Moderate** (consistent but small or mixed-quality studies), **Weak** (single or small studies, or poor-quality reviews), **None** (no usable evidence found).

| Claim | Evidence strength | How we may phrase it |
|---|---|---|
| Arts engagement is linked to wellbeing and social connection (population level) | Moderate to strong. WHO 2019 scoping review, Fancourt and Finn, over 900 publications in English and Russian, 2000–2019, per search summary (figure to verify; one result said 3,700 studies) ([WHO Health Evidence Network 67](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553773)) | "Creative activities are linked to enjoyment and connection." |
| Art therapy (as a professional treatment) improves mental health symptoms | Weak. Uttley et al. HTA (2015): 15 trials, 777 patients, mostly low to moderate quality, positive effects in 10 of 15. The BMC Psychiatry version: 11 RCTs, 533 patients, positive in 7 of 11. Harms noted: unresolved emotion, unskilled facilitation, abrupt endings | Only as a description of the profession's own claims, with citation. Never as our claim. |
| Coloring reduces anxiety | Moderate for anxiety in short samples. Curry and Kasser (2005), *Art Therapy* 22(2), n=84 students: mandala and structured-pattern coloring reduced anxiety more than free coloring. Flett et al. (2017), *Creativity Research Journal*, n=104 women, logic-puzzle control: lower depression and anxiety. Mantzios and Giannou (2018): no change in mindfulness or anxiety in some experiments | "Some people find coloring calming." Avoid "reduces anxiety" and "proven." |
| Coloring improves mindfulness | Weak. Mixed results across experiments | Do not claim. |
| Art-making lowers cortisol | Weak to moderate. Kaimal, Ray and Muniz (2016), *Art Therapy* 33(2), n=39 healthy adults, 45 minutes: cortisol fell in about three quarters and rose in about a quarter. Full text not reviewed | "Some studies have found a change in stress hormones after making art. Results vary." Do not give a percentage. |
| Music listening reduces anxiety | Moderate. A 2022 meta-analysis (k=21) reported a large effect (d = -0.77), mostly in non-clinical samples. Source not located precisely; verify before citing | "Listening to music you like can help you feel calmer for a while." |
| Music therapy helps depression and anxiety | Weak to moderate. 2024 transdiagnostic meta-review (music therapy, not listening) reported SMD 0.57 for depression and 0.47 for anxiety; depression reviews are methodologically weak | Not for our product. Refer to a music therapist. |
| Arts and culture programs help people with dementia | Weak to moderate. Letrondo et al. (2022) systematic review: possible benefits for people with dementia and caregivers; heterogeneous interventions and outcomes | "Many people with dementia enjoy making things together. Check with their care team." |
| Museum or arts programs reduce loneliness | Weak to moderate. Museums on Prescription pre-post result; small self-reported evaluations | "Some programs report that participants felt less isolated." |
| Arts-on-prescription improves health | Moderate for direction, weak for size. NASP 2022 review; evaluations still emerging | "Arts on prescription is being tested across the UK." |
| Journaling or morning pages improve wellbeing | Weak. Mostly observational and self-help literature | "A practice some people use to notice how they feel." |
| Creative activity helps grief | Weak. Weiskittle and Gramling (2018), 27 studies, mixed efficacy | "Some people find making something helpful when they are grieving. You can stop at any time." |

## 6. Gaps no one serves well

- **A clean line between "creative" and "therapy" for consumers.** Most apps use "art therapy" loosely; professional bodies reserve it. A site that uses the term only to explain the boundary would be unusual and trustworthy.
- **Crisis-aware creative activities.** I found no creative-activity app or site with a visible crisis route on its activity pages.
- **Printable, care-setting activities with safety labels.** App-based coloring dominates; print-first activities for care homes and families are sparse in the reviewed set.
- **Honest evidence labels on activity pages.** None of the apps reviewed showed evidence strength next to a benefit claim.
- **Activities for people with limited energy or mobility** that are not framed as rehabilitation. This overlaps with MISSION.md's underserved segments.

## 7. Anti-patterns and risks

- **Therapy vocabulary as marketing.** Happy Color's listing ("relaxation, stress relief, or art therapy") and Calm's blog ("a form of art therapy") show how easily the term spreads. Do not use "art therapy," "therapeutic," "healing," "treats," or "heals" for activities.
- **Regulatory exposure.** Health claims carry app-store and regulator risk. Apple's guideline 1.4.1 asks for extra scrutiny of medical apps; the FDA general wellness policy allows relaxation and "mental acuity" wording but not disease-specific treatment claims; MHRA guidance treats app-store copy as evidence of intended purpose. These points are carried from `docs/plan/competitors.md` and were not re-checked today ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download); [MHRA](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1105233/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps.pdf)).
- **Streaks and shame.** Finch's gamified pet and daily check-ins are the pattern to avoid for vulnerable users.
- **Disclosure traps.** Prompts such as "write about your loss" can surface distress without a facilitator. Keep these out of self-guided flows.
- **Trademark and copyright.** Do not use "Zentangle" in copy, and do not reproduce The Artist's Way exercises or Lake and Happy Color illustrations.
- **Ads.** Happy Color's ads between pictures are the reason several reviewers stopped using it. No ads in the activity flow.
- **Credential misuse.** Do not imply that an activity is supervised by an ATR, ATR-BC or HCPC-registered therapist unless one reviewed it. Name the reviewer and their credential only with written permission, and state their scope.

### Working with credentialed reviewers

- Invite a registered art therapist (ATR or ATR-BC in the US; HCPC-registered in the UK) to review any page that touches emotion, grief, trauma, mental health or dementia behavior. Their review does not make the activity a therapy.
- Ask the reviewer to state what the activity is not for, which people should not use it unsupervised, and the stop signals to watch for.
- Record reviewer name, credential, date and scope on the page, as DESIGN.md requires. Do not show a "clinically reviewed" badge unless the review is documented.

### Wording rules (summary)

- Say: creative, recreational, enjoyable, calming for some people, a way to make something together, a chance to notice how you feel.
- Avoid: therapy, therapeutic, healing, treats, heals, cures, improves memory, reduces anxiety, proven, scientifically shown.
- Every benefit sentence should be traceable to a row in the section 5 table, with a dated source.
