A group art session gives a small circle of people something to do together, at a pace that suits each person. This page is for activity coordinators, volunteers and families who run a table for three to eight people. It covers choosing a project, setting up the room, and giving everyone a part, including people who would rather watch.

One note on terms first. [Art therapy](/activities/art-therapy-or-creative-activity/) is a mental health profession practiced by trained therapists, many of whom hold credentials for that work. This page is about ordinary creative activities. Nothing here is therapy.

## How do you choose a group art activity?

Pick something that needs little skill, has few steps, and can be stopped at any point. The Alzheimer's Association's [activities guide](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) advises matching activities to people's abilities and past interests, keeping them simple, and focusing on the process rather than the result. Good choices use one table and materials you can clean up in a few minutes. For more on running groups in care settings, see the [care home groups guide](/activities/care-home-groups/) and the [activity ideas guide](/activities/ideas/).

## How should the room and table be set up?

- Seat people across from each other, or in a loose circle, so everyone can see the middle.
- Use one table for no more than four people. For eight, use two small tables side by side.
- Put each person's materials in the same place each time, so they learn where things are.
- Cover the table with a plastic cloth, and use even light without glare on the paper.
- Keep a clear path to the door, and offer a chair with arms for anyone who needs to stand up.
- Wash hands before and after, and keep a damp cloth close by.

## Should everyone make one piece or their own?

Both work. A shared piece, such as a paper chain or a mosaic, gives people a reason to talk and a result they made together. Individual pieces let each person set the pace. Many groups do both: each person makes a tile or card, and the tiles are joined into one display at the end. Ask before you display anyone's work, and show it only where that person would like it seen.

## What roles help people who prefer to watch?

Not everyone wants to hold a brush, and that is fine. Offer roles that are real rather than pretend:

- **Color chooser.** Hold up the jars and ask which color goes next.
- **Tray keeper.** Keep the spare paper and glue within reach of the group.
- **Reader.** Read out the next step, or a short poem between pieces.
- **Observer.** Watching and commenting on the work is a full role. Say so out loud so no one feels left out.

Give the observer a seat in the circle, not at the back of the room.

## How do you end the session calmly?

Give a clear warning before the end, in a calm voice: "We have about ten minutes left." Then let people finish a line or a fold, rather than stopping mid-stroke. Put finished work where everyone can see it, or let each person take their piece home. Thank the group for their time, not for the result.

Stopping early is fine. If someone becomes upset, pause, offer a quiet seat and a cup of tea, and try again another day. A session that ends with a short conversation and a sense of calm is a good session.
