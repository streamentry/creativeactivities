Art therapy and a creative art activity can look alike from the outside. Both use paper, paint and clay. They are different in purpose, in who leads them and in the training behind them. Knowing the difference helps you choose the right kind of support, and it keeps you from calling a fun afternoon of painting something it is not. This page explains the difference and what you can do at home today.

## What is art therapy?

Art therapy is a mental health profession. Art therapists are trained clinicians who use art-making within a professional relationship and work toward goals set with the person. Their work is usually part of a wider mental health or care plan.

In the United States, art therapists can earn credentials such as ATR or ATR-BC, which require graduate education and supervised experience. The [American Art Therapy Association](https://arttherapy.org/credentials-and-licensure/) explains those credentials. In the UK, "art therapist" and "art psychotherapist" are protected titles, and practitioners must be registered. The [British Association of Art Therapists](https://www.baat.org/art-therapy/what-is-art-therapy/) describes the profession.

## What is a creative art activity?

A creative art activity is something you do for enjoyment and company. There is no treatment plan, no assessment and no clinical goal. Painting a picture together, tearing paper into a mosaic or making a birthday card are all creative activities.

The activities on this site are ordinary activities of this kind. They are for time together, not for treatment. They do not treat, cure or prevent any condition, and they do not improve memory.

## How do art therapy and a creative activity compare?

| | Art therapy | A creative art activity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who leads it | A trained, credentialed art therapist | A family member, friend or activity leader |
| Main purpose | A clinical goal, set with a professional | Enjoyment, company and something to do |
| Training | Formal graduate training and supervised practice | No training needed |
| Relationship | A professional, clinical relationship | An everyday relationship |
| Where it happens | Clinics, hospitals, schools or private practices | Home, a care setting or a community space |
| Who can check it | Licensing or registration bodies | No one needs to check it |

## When should you look for an art therapist?

Talk to a doctor first. A doctor can tell you whether a referral to a licensed mental health professional makes sense. If someone you care for has ongoing anxiety, depression, trauma or other emotional difficulties, a professional can help in ways a craft table cannot.

For help finding and checking a therapist, read [how to find and check an art therapist](/activities/finding-an-art-therapist/). If a person talks about harming themselves, or you are worried about their safety, do not wait. Contact a doctor, a crisis line or emergency services right away.

## What can you do at home today?

You do not need a therapist to enjoy art together. Choose something simple, set it up in advance and stop whenever it stops being pleasant. These are good places to begin:

- [Paint with watercolors](/activity/paint-with-watercolors/), letting the colors run on wet paper.
- [Make a torn paper mosaic](/activity/tear-paper-mosaic/), with no scissors and no right way to place the pieces.
- [Make a memory collage](/activity/make-a-memory-collage/) from photos and magazine pictures.
- [Press leaves and flowers](/activity/press-leaves-and-flowers/) between the pages of a heavy book.

For more ideas, see our [arts and crafts guide](/activities/arts-and-crafts/). Art can be a pleasant part of your week. It does not have to be called therapy to be worth doing.
