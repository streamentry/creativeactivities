Many adults say they cannot draw, paint or make things. Often they mean they have not done it since school, or that they worry the result will look childish or poor. This page is for anyone who wants to make something with another person and has no art training to offer. The projects below are simple, use everyday materials, and have no wrong result.

The crafts here work for a grandparent, a friend, a carer or a volunteer. They also suit someone who has less energy than they used to, someone who prefers to sit, or someone who simply wants a calm hour with their hands busy. These are suggestions based on what the activity asks for, not a judgment about what anyone can do.

## What do you need?

Most of these crafts need only household items and a little from a shop:

- A table with a plastic cloth or old sheet over it
- Heavy paper or thin card, such as a cereal box, a paper plate or a folded sheet of printer paper
- A glue stick, which is easier to use than liquid glue
- A few watercolor paints or food-safe dyes, a large soft brush and two small cups of water
- Pictures from old magazines, greeting cards or seed packets
- Dried or pressed flowers, leaves or a few stems from the garden

Our [art supplies kit guide](/activities/art-supplies-for-older-adults/) lists ten low-cost items if you are starting from nothing.

## Which crafts are easiest to begin?

These projects each have one clear first step. Choose one, and keep to it for the session.

### Color collage

Choose a single color and tear pictures of that color from magazines. Tearing is easier than cutting, and it has no sharp tools. Glue the pieces onto card together. The [color collage card](/activity/make-a-color-collage/) has the full steps.

### Watercolor washes

Wet a sheet of heavy paper with clean water, then let the person drop color onto it with a large brush. The colors spread and run together. There is nothing to get wrong. The [watercolor card](/activity/paint-with-watercolors/) covers paper, paint and cleanup.

### A folded card

Fold a sheet of card in half. Glue on one picture, then write a short message together for someone the person cares about. Ask who the card is for before you start, since a real reason to make something makes it more enjoyable. The [greeting card card](/activity/make-a-greeting-card/) gives the steps.

### Flowers in a jar

Trim a few stems to length and place them one at a time in a heavy jar. Stand back and look at the arrangement together. The [flower arranging card](/activity/arrange-flowers-together/) has easier versions for a single stem or a small cup.

### Pressed leaves and flowers

Collect a few leaves or small flowers, press them between the pages of a heavy book for a week, then glue them onto a folded card. The [pressing card](/activity/press-leaves-and-flowers/) explains the steps.

### A texture board

Glue scraps of fabric, ribbon and paper onto a stiff card. Touching the finished board is part of the activity, so it suits people who like to handle things. See the [texture board card](/activity/make-a-texture-board/).

### Soft clay

Shape a small bowl from air-dry clay by pressing with the thumbs. It needs no tools, and it dries overnight. See the [pinch pot card](/activity/shape-a-pinch-pot/).

## How do you run a session?

1. **Set up first.** Lay out one or two materials, a cloth and a damp cloth for hands. Cut and tear anything you need in advance.
2. **Show the first step.** Dip the brush, tear the first piece or press the first leaf, and invite the person to do the same.
3. **Work side by side.** Keep your own piece close, so the activity feels shared rather than supervised.
4. **Talk about what you see.** Comment on color, texture and shape. Avoid quiz questions such as "What is that?" and praise such as "Good job", which can feel childish.
5. **Stop before the energy drops.** Ten to twenty minutes is often enough. The project can be finished another day, or left as it is.

The Alzheimer's Association [notes](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/art-music) that art projects can create a sense of accomplishment and purpose, and give an opportunity for self-expression. The NHS [activities page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) notes that activities often work best when they are simple and use the senses, such as touch and sight.

## What easier versions can you try?

Every craft above can be made easier. Pick the change that fits the person today.

- **Seated.** All of these work at a table. Sit side by side, not standing over the person.
- **Easier to grip.** Use chunky brushes, jumbo crayons or a glue stick with a wide body. Wrap a thin handle in foam tape. Tape the paper down so it does not slide.
- **Low vision.** Use bright paper with a dark outline on the picture, one large color at a time, and a lamp over the table. Make the finished sample visible.
- **Fewer choices.** Offer two colors, or four pictures already torn. Let the person choose between them rather than from a whole table of materials.
- **Shorter.** Pre-glue the first piece, so the person only adds the last one. A card with one picture can be finished in five minutes.

## Safety notes

- Use non-toxic materials, and check that paints and glue are labelled for the purpose. The [Art & Creative Materials Institute](https://www.acmiart.org/classroom-art-materials-safety) explains the AP seal, which marks art materials certified as containing nothing in sufficient quantities to be toxic. Check the label, not just the color of the package.
- Keep scissors blunt or use tearing instead. Do any cutting with a craft knife yourself, ahead of time.
- Watch for anyone putting paint, clay, flowers or small pieces in their mouth. Keep loose pieces in a tray.
- Check for latex in gloves and for any known skin sensitivity to glue, dye or plant sap.
- Some plants and seeds are not safe to handle or taste. Use only plants you can identify.

## If it isn't working

- **The person pushes the materials away.** Stop, and put the project somewhere safe. Offer a cup of tea or a walk. Trying again another day is a normal choice.
- **Frustration shows.** Take the hard step yourself and invite them to do the next one. A smaller project, or one with a pre-made piece, can help.
- **They say they cannot do it.** Reassure them that the result does not matter. Start the first stroke and leave space for them to continue. If they still decline, let it go.
- **You are not sure what to do next.** Keep to one material and one step. Simple is fine.

## Doing it with someone living with dementia

Crafts like these can suit an older adult, including someone living with dementia. One of the most useful changes is to frame the task as a real job, such as making a card for a friend. Keep the steps very short, and let the person do the part they can still manage, such as choosing colors or pressing a leaf down. In the later stages, a watercolor wash or a clay pinch may be enough to take part in. Watching you make your own piece, and talking about the colors, also counts. For more on this, see our [group art activities guide](/activities/group-art-activities/).

When the person no longer wants to join in, respect that. Our guide on [when someone says no](/caregiving/when-they-say-no/) explains how to step back without pressure.

Creative Activities is not therapy. Art therapy is a separate, clinical profession that a qualified person provides. These crafts are for enjoyment and shared time.
