Coloring is an easy creative activity to offer an adult. It needs no reading, no memory and no right answer. The hands move, colors come out, and there is a picture to talk about. Many adults who say they cannot draw will still color, because the lines are already there and the choices are theirs. This page covers how to choose designs, what to set out, how to color together, and how to use the coloring game on this site.

Coloring suits adults who enjoy making things at a table, people who have less energy than they used to, and anyone who wants a short, calm activity to share. It can also be done on your own, in a waiting room or in bed. These are suggestions based on what the activity needs. They do not describe what any one person can or cannot do.

## What do you need?

- **Thick, easy-to-hold tools.** Jumbo crayons, chunky markers and colored pencils with a wide body are easier than thin pens.
- **A small palette.** Three or four colors are enough. More colors can make the choice feel bigger than it is.
- **Sturdy paper or card.** Thin paper tears and slides. Tape the corners down, or use a clipboard.
- **A clear table.** Set out one page at a time.
- **Something to blend with.** A soft cloth, a cotton bud or a sponge gives a softer effect.

## How do you choose a design?

Adults often dislike pages that look too childish. Look for pages with real subjects and large, simple areas:

- **Nature.** A garden with big flowers, a lake with a boat, birds on a branch.
- **Familiar objects.** A teapot, a bicycle, a bowl of fruit, a hat.
- **Large patterns.** Bold geometric shapes or a mandala with a few large sections.
- **Simple landscapes.** A hill, a field or a window view.

Avoid pages with many tiny, crowded areas. They are frustrating to fill, and the person may stop before they have enjoyed the picture. A page with a few large shapes is often better than a detailed one. Choose a design that suits the person's own taste. Someone who loved gardening may enjoy flowers, and someone who loved boats may enjoy a harbor scene.

The [printable pictures page](/pictures/) has friendly drawings of everyday things, such as a teapot, a dog, a rose or a red bus. Print a few at a time on sturdy paper, so there is a choice without a pile.

## Coloring on a tablet

The [coloring game](/games/coloring/) on this site works by tapping a color, then tapping a part of the picture. It needs no pencil and no fine hand control, so it can suit someone whose grip has weakened. Sit beside the person so you can share the screen. Let them choose the colors, and let them tap as often as they wish. Turn off notifications on the device so the picture stays on the screen.

A tablet suits a quiet afternoon, a bed or a chair by a window. It also has no mess. Some people prefer paper, and that is fine. Use whichever the person enjoys.

## How do you color together?

1. **Begin the first stroke together.** Hold the crayon with the person, or put your hand over theirs for a few strokes, then let go.
2. **Talk about color and place.** "That blue is lovely. Where have you seen water that color?" invites conversation without a quiz.
3. **Praise the choices, not the neatness.** "You chose such warm colors" feels better than "You stayed in the lines."
4. **Let them lead.** If they want to scribble over the whole page, let them. It is still coloring.
5. **Stop when they stop.** Put the crayons down, and suggest a cup of tea or a short walk.

Coloring side by side can feel shared rather than supervised. Work on your own page next to theirs.

## What easier versions can you try?

- **Seated.** Coloring is a seated activity, so set the table at a height that suits the person and their chair.
- **Easier to grip.** Use jumbo crayons or markers, or wrap a thin pencil in foam tape to make it thicker. A clipboard can be held on the lap.
- **Low vision.** Choose thick outlines with high contrast, and use one bold color at a time. A good lamp over the table helps.
- **Fewer choices.** Offer two colors, or one page with the colors already chosen. Let the person pick a single color for one large area.
- **Shorter.** Ask for one shape, or a single corner of the page. Ending after two minutes is a good session.
- **Nonverbal.** Hold up two pages, or two colors, and watch for a reach or a look. Pointing and nodding are answers.

## Safety notes

- Use non-toxic crayons, markers and colored pencils. Check that the label says non-toxic. The [Art & Creative Materials Institute](https://www.acmiart.org/classroom-art-materials-safety) explains what the AP seal means for art materials.
- Keep sharpeners and small caps away from anyone who might put them in their mouth.
- For a person who mouths objects, use larger, non-toxic pieces, and check them often.
- A tablet should stay out of the water and away from food and drink.

## If it isn't working

- **The page is too busy.** Swap it for a simpler design with larger areas.
- **Frustration shows.** Put the crayon down, and offer to do the next area together. Or stop, and come back another day.
- **The person pushes the page away.** That is a clear answer. Offer a different activity, such as a conversation or a walk.
- **They want to color, but not with the tool.** Try a thick marker, a chunky crayon or a finger dipped in watercolor.

## Doing it with someone living with dementia

Coloring is often offered to older adults, including people living with dementia, because it needs no memory and no right answer. In the early stages, many people enjoy detailed pages and choose their own colors. In the middle stage, larger pictures and fewer choices help. In the later stages, a single large shape, a soft-colored sheet, or simply holding a crayon while you hum a song can be enough. Some people enjoy looking at a finished picture more than making one.

Activities work best when they match what a person can still do, and when they are adjusted as abilities change. The NHS [activities page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) notes that later-stage activities often focus on the senses. For more ideas, see our [late-stage activities guide](/activities/late-stage/).

If you are looking for a calm game to follow a coloring session, try [picture bingo](/games/picture-bingo/), which uses clear pictures rather than numbers.

Creative Activities is not therapy. Coloring is an activity to enjoy together, not a treatment.
