Paper is one of the easiest materials to make with. It folds, tears, glues and stacks, and it costs very little. It also needs no sharp tools, which makes it a good choice when you want a craft that is safe for a mixed group and easy to tidy up afterward. This page covers the paper crafts we have on the site, how to set them up, and how to adapt them.

The projects here suit adults who want something simple to make, families with older children, and groups at a library or a club. They also suit someone who prefers to sit and work with their hands, or who would rather not use scissors. These are suggestions for the activity, not a judgment about anyone.

## What do you need?

- Thick paper or card. Cereal boxes, greeting cards and paper plates all work. Thin printer paper is fine for folding but tears more easily.
- A glue stick, or double-sided tape, for work that needs to hold without drying time.
- Tissue paper or old magazine pages for tearing.
- A tray with raised edges, so small pieces stay in one place.
- Masking tape to hold the paper still on the table.
- Blunt safety scissors, if you use scissors at all. A craft knife is for adults only, and is not needed for any project here.

## Which paper crafts are easy to begin?

### Fold a paper boat

Fold one sheet of paper into a simple boat, then float it in a basin of water. Folding uses only the hands, and the result floats, which many people find satisfying. The [paper boat card](/activity/fold-a-paper-boat/) gives the steps, with an easier version and a group version.

### Make a paper chain

Cut or tear strips of colored paper, loop each one through the last, and glue or tape the ends. A paper chain grows one link at a time and can be hung in a room, for a party or for a season. The [paper chain garland card](/activity/make-a-paper-chain-garland/) explains how to set it up for a group.

### Weave paper strips

Weave colored strips over and under a card base that has already been slit. The rhythm of weaving is calm and repetitive, and the finished piece is bright. The [weaving card](/activity/weave-paper-strips/) covers the base and the strips.

### Tear-paper mosaic

Tear colored paper into pieces, then glue them onto card to make a simple picture. There is no drawing and no scissors. The [tear-paper mosaic card](/activity/tear-paper-mosaic/) gives steps for one person or a small group.

### Make a greeting card

Fold card in half, glue one picture on the front, and write a short message inside. This is one of the most useful paper crafts, because the card has a real destination. See the [greeting card card](/activity/make-a-greeting-card/).

### Color collage

Choose one color and tear pictures of that color from magazines. Glue them onto card in any arrangement. The [color collage card](/activity/make-a-color-collage/) has setup steps and a version for low vision.

## How do you set up a paper session?

1. **Pre-score the first fold.** Run a blunt pen or the back of a spoon along the line, so the first fold is easy.
2. **Tape the paper down.** A strip of masking tape across the top corners keeps the sheet still for one-handed work.
3. **Sort the pieces before you start.** Put torn or cut pieces into a tray, grouped by color. This gives the person a clear choice without a mess.
4. **Show one step.** Fold the first corner, or tear the first piece, and let the person copy it.
5. **Stop at a good point.** Finish one chain link, one fold or one card, and then pause.

## What easier versions can you try?

- **Seated.** All of these work at a table. Sit alongside rather than across.
- **Easier to grip.** Use thicker card, which holds its shape and is easier to pick up. Tape in place of folding, so the paper stays flat.
- **One-handed.** Tape the paper to the table and use a stand-alone tray. Glue sticks can be operated with one hand once the lid is off.
- **Low vision.** Use strong contrast, such as a dark card under white paper, and one large picture per card.
- **Fewer choices.** Offer two colors or three pre-cut shapes. A pre-made sample to copy makes the next step easy.
- **Shorter.** Pre-fold the boat, or glue the chain's first two links, so the person only adds a few.

## Safety notes

- Keep small pieces of paper, confetti and tissue scraps in a tray or a bin with a lid. Pieces this size can be a choking hazard for children under three. The [HealthyChildren.org choking prevention page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/pages/choking-prevention.aspx) lists small parts among the common causes of choking in young children.
- Use blunt safety scissors for children, and keep craft knives with adults only.
- Paper edges can cut fingers, especially on dry hands. Some people prefer to tear rather than cut.
- Check glue labels for skin sensitivities. Use a glue stick or a non-toxic craft glue.
- Do not let anyone put paper or glue in their mouth.

## If it isn't working

- **The folds keep slipping.** Use thicker paper, or tape the fold. A pre-scored crease helps a lot.
- **The strips are too thin.** Cut wider strips, or use a thicker paper. Thin strips are hard to hold.
- **The person gets tired or bored.** Stop after one piece. You can always come back to the project another day.
- **Pieces keep going on the floor.** Work in a tray, and place the tray on a stable table.

## Doing it with an older adult or a child

Many of these crafts suit older adults and children working together. For an older adult, including someone living with dementia, keep the steps short and let them do the part they still enjoy, such as choosing colors or pressing a fold flat. For a child, always keep an adult close, use larger pieces, and keep small items out of reach of toddlers. Paper crafts are about enjoying the making. Children may enjoy the activity, and the result can be a card or a boat they keep.
