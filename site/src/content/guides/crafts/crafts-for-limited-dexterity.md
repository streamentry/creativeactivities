Grip, strength and fine control can change for many reasons. Arthritis, a recent illness, an injury, a long period in bed and the normal changes of getting older can each make a pen, a pair of scissors or a small piece of paper harder to manage. A craft can still be enjoyed, and the right setup often matters more than the project. This page covers the adaptations that work across many crafts, and the cards on this site chosen because they need few fine movements.

These suggestions are for people who want to make something and find some hand tasks difficult. They are not a diagnosis, and they are not medical advice. If holding a tool causes pain, stop. A doctor, a physiotherapist or an occupational therapist can advise on hand health and on equipment.

## What setup helps most?

The biggest difference often comes from how the materials are arranged, not from the project itself.

- **Tape the paper.** Use masking tape across the corners of a sheet, so it stays flat without a second hand.
- **Use a non-slip mat or a damp cloth** under the work, so the board or tray does not slide.
- **Use a tray with raised edges.** Pieces stay in the tray, and the person can pick them up from one place.
- **Keep everything in reach.** Place the tools in front of the person, on the side of their stronger hand, so they do not need to reach far.
- **Pre-cut and pre-prepare.** Cut, fold and set out the pieces before the person sits down, so the work is in the making and not the preparation.

## How can you make tools easier to hold?

- **Build up the handle.** Wrap a thin pen, brush or pencil with foam tape, or slide a foam tube over it. A thicker handle needs less pinch.
- **Use wide tools.** Jumbo crayons, chunky markers and large brushes with short, wide handles are easier to hold than thin ones.
- **Try a palm grip.** Some people find a tool held in the whole hand easier than a tool held between the fingers. Test it before you start.
- **Choose a different tool.** A glue stick may be easier than a bottle of glue. A sponge or a roller may be easier than a brush. A stamp may be easier than drawing.
- **Use tape instead of tying.** Tape or a clip can hold a ribbon, a card or a strip in place with one hand.

The [Arthritis Foundation](https://www.arthritis.org/partnership/ease-of-use) runs an Ease of Use program that certifies everyday products as easier to use, including joint protection accessories and easy-open products. Its label is a useful starting point when you shop, but it does not replace trying the product with the person first.

## Which crafts need fewer fine movements?

Choose projects with larger movements, a palm grip or a single, clear action. These cards on this site suit people with limited grip, and each has an easier version:

- [Tear-paper mosaic](/activity/tear-paper-mosaic/). Tearing is done with the fingers, with no scissors. The card has easier and bedside versions.
- [Arrange flowers together](/activity/arrange-flowers-together/). Trimmed stems placed in a wide, stable jar. The card includes an easier version and a bedside version.
- [Tap a rhythm together](/activity/tap-a-rhythm-together/). Tapping with a whole hand on a table or tray, with no tool needed.
- [Make a texture board](/activity/make-a-texture-board/). Fabric and paper pieces glued onto card, with a clear first step.
- [Make a greeting card](/activity/make-a-greeting-card/). A card can be pre-folded, with one picture already glued on, so the only job is the message.
- [Make a sealed shaker](/activity/make-a-sealed-shaker/). A helper seals the lid, so the person only fills and decorates.
- [Make a lavender sachet](/activity/make-a-lavender-sachet/). It needs no sewing, and the card has easier and bedside versions.

Some cards involve tiny pieces, such as buttons or beads. The [sort buttons by color card](/activity/sort-buttons-by-color/) uses large buttons in trays, and it is for adults and older children only, since buttons are a choking hazard for under-threes.

## What easier versions can you try?

- **Seated or bedside.** Most of these cards work seated or in bed. Set the tray on a stable surface, at a height that does not need reaching.
- **Easier to grip.** Use built-up tools and wide, lightweight containers. Choose lids that twist easily or pop open.
- **One-handed.** Use tape, a clamp or a non-slip mat, and a tray with raised edges. A pre-loaded brush or a pre-spread glue stick helps.
- **Fewer choices.** Offer two colors or two pieces, and let the person choose from those.
- **Shorter.** Ask for one piece, one stem or one card. A short session that ends well is a good session.
- **Less mess.** Work on a tray with a cloth underneath. Choose dry crafts such as tearing or arranging.

## Safety notes

- Keep small pieces, such as buttons, beads and seeds, in a tray. Under-threes can choke on them.
- Use blunt scissors or ready-cut pieces. Craft knives are for adults only.
- Choose non-toxic, labelled paints and glues. The [Art & Creative Materials Institute](https://www.acmiart.org/classroom-art-materials-safety) explains the AP seal on art materials.
- Check that a grip aid, such as foam tape, does not slip, trap fingers or come loose.
- Stop if the person has pain, numbness or swelling after an activity, and speak to a doctor or physiotherapist about it.

## If it isn't working

- **Holding the tool hurts.** Stop, and try a wider or lighter tool. Or choose a different activity.
- **The paper keeps moving.** Add more tape, or clamp the corner to the table.
- **The pieces keep falling.** Use a tray with higher edges, or a smaller number of pieces.
- **The person is tired.** Stop after one piece. The project can be finished another day, or left as it is.
- **Nothing seems to work.** Choose a project with less fine movement, such as tapping a rhythm or looking at a window together.

## Doing it with an older adult or someone living with dementia

Many of the adaptations above suit older adults, including people living with dementia, who may also have limited grip. Break the craft into small steps, and let the person do the parts they can manage, such as choosing the colors or pressing a piece down. Do the fine work yourself, and let the person decide how much of the craft they want to take part in.

Creative Activities is not therapy, and these adaptations are not a treatment. They make a craft easier to try, and a person can always stop.
