A group craft session can bring people together around a table, whether the table is in a library, a care home, a club, a classroom or a front room at a family gathering. The group does not need an expert leader or special equipment. It needs a plan that is familiar, short and easy for anyone to join, and a leader who makes it clear that every contribution counts. This page covers how to plan and run that kind of session, and how to adapt one craft for people with mixed abilities.

These sessions suit libraries running a monthly craft table, activity staff in a care home, volunteers at a community club, teachers with a mixed class, and families who want to make something together at a gathering. They are for people who enjoy company and making things, and for the leaders who support them. The suggestions describe what makes a group work well. They are not a judgment about any person in the group.

## What makes a group craft work?

A good group craft is familiar, short, easy to join at any level and free of pressure. In practice that means:

- **Familiar beats new.** Choose a craft people already know, or one that uses materials they recognize, such as paper, paint or a picture of something from their past.
- **Short beats long.** Plan for the attention span of the group. Build in a natural stopping point partway through.
- **Doing beats watching.** Give each person something to hold, tear, tap, choose or say.
- **Many levels, one activity.** Plan easier and harder ways to take part, so nobody is left out.
- **No winners and no scores.** Competitive formats leave some people feeling they failed. Shared projects avoid this.

The Alzheimer's Association's [dementia care practice recommendations](https://www.alz.org/getmedia/1a0020aa-0bfb-47ac-a545-1dd8a3ee1444/alzheimers-dementia-care-practice-recommendations.pdf) are a useful reference for leaders working in care settings.

## How big should the group be?

Smaller is usually easier. A group of four to eight people with one leader and a helper for each table is a good starting point. A person who finds a large gathering overwhelming may take part more easily in a smaller group.

A useful test: can every person see the leader's face, hear clearly and be reached by a helper within a few seconds? If not, split the group, add helpers, or move to a smaller room. For a large event, seat helpers among the group rather than along the walls.

## How should you set up the room?

- **A table or circle.** People sit close enough to share materials, with the leader at one end or within the circle.
- **Leader close, at eye level.** Sit rather than stand when you can.
- **Hearing and sight first.** Seat anyone with hearing loss nearest the leader, on their better side. Check that glasses and hearing aids are on.
- **Light from the side or the front.** Avoid seating anyone with their back to a bright window.
- **Turn off competing noise.** Switch off the television or radio, and keep background sound low.
- **One conversation at a time.** Crosstalk is hard to follow for many people, so the leader can ask one person to speak at a time.
- **A clear exit and a quiet corner.** Anyone who wants to leave should be able to do so easily. Keep a quiet place nearby, with a helper who can walk with them.

## Crafts that work in a group

These projects have a shared result, so each person adds a piece. The cards below have full steps and easier versions:

- [Paint a shared picture](/activity/paint-a-shared-picture/). One large picture on a sheet taped to the table, with everyone adding in turn. It suits small groups and intergenerational groups.
- [Make a paper chain garland](/activity/make-a-paper-chain-garland/). Each person makes links, and the whole group hangs the chain in the room. It is easy to make for many people at once.
- [Tear-paper mosaic](/activity/tear-paper-mosaic/). Everyone tears colored paper and glues it onto a shared card. There are no scissors.
- [Make a family tree of hands](/activity/make-a-family-tree-of-hands/). Each person traces a hand in pencil around a drawn trunk. There is no paint on skin, so it suits all ages.
- [Press leaves and flowers](/activity/press-leaves-and-flowers/). A group can collect, sort and press leaves together, then make cards.
- [Make a personal songbook](/activity/make-a-personal-songbook/). Each person names songs they love and the stories behind them. It works well for conversation as well as making.
- [Tap a rhythm together](/activity/tap-a-rhythm-together/). Everyone taps along to a steady song, taking turns to lead.

## How do you adapt one craft for mixed abilities?

Plan at least three ways to join every activity. The table below shows how a shared paint picture can work at three levels:

| Level | What the person does | What the helper does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More independent | Chooses a color and paints a section | Keeps the paints in reach and talks about the picture |
| Some support | Dabs a sponge with a color the helper has chosen | Guides the sponge and places the sheet |
| Most support | Chooses between two colors, or watches and comments | Holds the brush or sponge and paints the section together |

The same approach works for a paper chain (make a whole link, glue one link, or hold the chain as it grows), a mosaic (tear a piece, place a piece, or choose the color), and a songbook (write a title, say a title, or point to one).

## How should leaders speak to a group?

- Introduce yourself each time, and use the name each person prefers.
- Speak slowly, give one step at a time, and show before you tell.
- Ask open invitations such as "Tell me about this," or simple choices. Avoid quiz questions such as "Do you remember?"
- Do not correct people. Thank every contribution in the same way.
- Keep the same opening and closing words each time, so the session feels familiar.

## How do you end well?

Stopping is a good outcome. Watch for signs that someone is tired, restless or upset. Have a helper offer a walk or a quiet seat somewhere else, and let people leave without comment. Someone who dozes during a session may simply be comfortable.

Close every group the same way, with a familiar song or a simple thank you, so the ending feels calm and expected. Afterward, note what worked and who enjoyed what. Those notes make the next session better. Keep any finished work with the person who made it, and ask before displaying it.

## Doing it in a care home or memory care setting

In a care home or memory care setting, the same principles apply, with a few extra steps. Choose crafts that rely on the senses and familiar objects, since the NHS [activities page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) notes that activities often need to be simplified and can focus on touch, sight, sound and smell in the later stages. A one-to-one moment during or after a group may suit someone better than the group itself. Ask families what each person enjoyed, and use those things as the starting point.

Creative Activities is not therapy. A group craft session is a way for people to make something and spend time together.
