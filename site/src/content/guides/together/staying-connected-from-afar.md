A call from far away can be the highlight of someone's week, and it can also feel strained. People often ask "How are you?" and "What did you do today?", which asks the person to remember and report. This page offers a different approach. Plan one small thing to share, and let the call be about that. It covers video and phone calls, letters, recorded messages and shared projects by post, and then looks at how a relative far away can support the person who lives nearby.

## Activities for a video call

Video works best when there is a clear thing to look at or do together.

- **Show and tell.** Walk the camera slowly around your garden, introduce the dog, show what you are cooking, or hold up a drawing. Describe what you see in simple words and leave pauses for a reaction.
- **Look at photos.** Hold a print up to the camera, or share your screen and show a few large photos one at a time. Say, "Look at Grandpa's hat," rather than "Who is this?"
- **Read aloud.** A short poem, a psalm, a favorite passage or a letter from a relative gives the person something calm to listen to.
- **Sing or listen.** Sing a song they know, or play a favorite record at your end. Singing along is often easier than talking.
- **Cook the same recipe.** Set up the call in both kitchens. The person nearby can stir or taste while you follow their old recipe card.
- **Watch something at the same time.** A short clip, an old television episode or a game can be watched from separate homes while you talk.

If the person is nearby, a helper can open a game on a tablet and tap for them while you chat. Games that work well on a shared screen include [finish the saying](/games/finish-the-saying/), [picture pairs](/games/picture-pairs/), [conversation cards](/games/conversation-cards/) and [photo pairs](/games/photo-pairs/), which uses photos you choose on your own device.

## Phone calls

The Dementia Australia [visiting guide](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-connected/visiting-someone-dementia) suggests calling at the same time each day, and not rushing, since people may take time to engage or answer. On the phone:

- Say your name and your relationship straight away: "Hi Dad, it's Jenny, your daughter."
- Speak slowly, in short sentences, and ask one thing at a time.
- Describe something you are doing, such as cooking or walking to the post box.
- End while it still feels pleasant, and say when you will call again.

## Letters and recorded messages

Letters keep working when calls are hard. A letter can be read again later, at a calm moment, and a helper can read it aloud. [Write a letter together](/activity/write-a-letter-together/) turns a letter into a shared project: each person adds a line, a drawing or a pressed leaf. [Make a photo postcard](/activity/make-a-photo-postcard/) is a short message with a picture on the front.

When live calls are too tiring, record a short video: a greeting, a song, a walk around the yard. The person nearby can play it at a calm moment, as often as they enjoy it.

## Shared projects by post

A project that goes back and forth gives both people something to look forward to.

- **A songbook.** Each person chooses songs and writes a few lines about why they matter. See [make a personal songbook](/activity/make-a-personal-songbook/).
- **A recipe book.** The person nearby tells a family recipe and the story behind it, and you add your version. See [start a family recipe book](/activity/start-a-family-recipe-book/).
- **A family tree of hands.** Each person traces their hand on paper and posts it to the other, to be joined into one tree. See [make a family tree of hands](/activity/make-a-family-tree-of-hands/).

## Helping from a distance

If you live far away, you may be able to help in practical ways, if the person wants you to. Start by asking what they would like and who they want involved. Then consider these roles:

- **Appointments.** Help keep a calendar or plan transport, if the person agrees.
- **Research.** Look for local services, such as meal delivery, transport or adult day programs. Check details with each provider, since availability differs by area.
- **Paperwork.** Organize forms only with the person's agreement. Health and money records need extra care. Providers may need a signed release before they will share information with a relative, so ask them what they require.
- **Time for the local helper.** Offer to visit or to cover a task so the person nearby can rest.

The Alzheimer's Association [long-distance caregiving guide](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/long-distance-caregiving) describes many of these roles. Agree on one routine contact who sends ordinary updates, name a backup, and decide how urgent concerns will be raised. Do not add people to a care conversation just because they live nearby.

## With someone living with dementia

For a person living with dementia, keep calls short and familiar. Use the same time and the same greeting, and let them lead if they want to talk about something else. If the person does not seem to recognize you on screen, say your name and what you are doing, without asking them to work it out. The shared activity still counts.

## Safety and privacy

- Ask before you share photos of other people, and do not post a family member's health details online.
- Check that the person has given permission before anyone else joins a call or receives updates.
- Keep video calls in a quiet room, with the camera angle set so the person can see your face and your hands.

## If it is not working

Shorten the call, switch to audio only, or try a letter instead. If a call goes badly, end it warmly and try again on another day. The local helper can sit beside the person during the next call and help them join in.
