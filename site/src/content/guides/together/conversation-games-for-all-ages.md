Many families want a game that feels like a conversation rather than a test. Twenty questions and similar guessing games work well, because the rules are simple and the talk can go anywhere. The main idea is to reverse the usual roles. The person chooses the answer and you ask the questions. This page covers that game, finish-the-saying, open questions about the past and a few easy choice questions. It works for adults of any age, and for grandchildren who are old enough to play.

## Why reverse the usual roles?

In a typical quiz, one person asks and the other must know. That can leave an older person feeling put on the spot, especially if they find remembering hard. When they choose the object and you do the guessing, they are the one who knows. They decide when to say yes or no, and they can enjoy watching you try.

Keep sentences short and ask one question at a time. Speak in a calm voice and wait for the reply. The Alzheimer's Association's [activities page](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) advises sticking with activities the person has always enjoyed and adjusting them to match what they can do now. A conversation game is easy to adjust.

## How to play twenty questions

1. **Pick a theme** from the person's life, such as the kitchen, the garden, animals or the seaside.
2. **Let them think of something** and say it quietly, or point to a picture card so you do not need to hear it.
3. **Ask one yes-or-no question at a time.** "Is it something you eat?" "Is it bigger than a shoe?"
4. **Accept any answer.** A nod, a shake of the head or a laugh all count.
5. **Make your guesses out loud** and enjoy them. If you get it right, celebrate together.
6. **Offer a hint** when they seem stuck: "It is something in the kitchen."
7. **Never count the questions** and never keep score.

Good things to choose include a favorite fruit or flower, an animal from a farm or a pet they had, a tool such as a rake or a sewing needle, a piece of furniture, a place they know well, or a piece of clothing they liked to wear. Avoid names of people and anything that might bring up sadness.

## How to play finish-the-saying

A finish-the-saying game reads the first half of a familiar phrase and leaves the ending open. The words are old and well known, so the person does not need to remember anything about today.

1. **Sit at eye level,** facing the person, in a quiet moment.
2. **Read the first part slowly,** then pause.
3. **Wait.** Give it several seconds. If nothing comes, offer two endings, one of them correct.
4. **Celebrate any answer.** "Yes, that is the one," or "I had not thought of that," keeps it friendly.
5. **Talk about the saying.** Ask what it meant to them, or where they heard it. This is often the best part.
6. **Stop before they tire.** A few sayings and a laugh is a full session.

Some sayings to try: "Rain before seven..." (fine by eleven); "Too many cooks..." (spoil the broth); "Home is where the heart..." (is); "April showers bring..." (May flowers). Use the person's own favorites when you can. For the free online version, see [finish the saying](/games/finish-the-saying/).

## Open questions about the past

Reminiscence means talking together about earlier life: a childhood home, a first job, a favorite song or a family holiday. The key is to invite a story and never test a memory.

- Ask "Tell me about..." or "What was it like...?"
- Avoid "Who is this?", "What year was that?" or "Do you remember the name of...?"
- Accept whatever comes back. If the details change, enjoy the story and ask about the feeling.
- If a memory turns painful, do not push. Say, "That sounds like it was a hard time. Would you like to talk about something happier?" Then move on with a cup of tea or a favorite song.

Good openers include: What did your family eat on a Sunday? What songs did you dance to when you were young? What was your first home like? For more prompts, see [reminiscence](/activities/reminiscence/).

## Easy choice questions, not a quiz

This is not a quiz. Nobody is tested, scored or called on, and any answer is welcome. Offer two or three choices after a pause, read each question aloud and show it in large print.

| Question | Choices | Talk about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which instrument has 88 keys? | Guitar, **piano**, trumpet | Did anyone in your family play? |
| Who starred as Lucy in "I Love Lucy"? | **Lucille Ball**, Doris Day, Betty White | What shows did you watch together? |
| Which company made the Model T? | **Ford**, Chevrolet, Buick | Tell me about a car you liked. |

If nobody knows the answer, say it warmly and move on. Stories are the real goal. Keep to about ten questions.

## Other easy conversation games

- **This or that.** Offer two options and ask which they prefer: tea or coffee, the mountains or the sea. There is no wrong answer.
- **Would you rather.** A light question such as "Would you rather have a picnic or dinner out?"
- **Name the place.** Ask them to describe a favorite room, town or view, and invite you to picture it too.
- **Open prompt cards.** The [conversation cards](/games/conversation-cards/) give prompts by theme, and can be used at the table.

## Doing these with someone living with dementia

These games were shaped with dementia in mind, but they suit many people. Keep to one question at a time, and give more time for an answer. If the person cannot find words, switch to a gesture or a picture card. A familiar sentence said together, with a hand on theirs, can still be a shared moment. Pair the game with music or a warm drink if that helps.

Watch for frustration. If the person looks tense or upset, stop and say, "Let's do something else." Changing the game is a good response, not a failure. The guide on [how to play games together](/caregiving/how-to-play-together/) explains how to change the rules on the spot.

## If it is not working

Shorten the round, change the theme or try a different game. Some days the best conversation is a few words about the weather. Stopping early is fine, and you can come back to a game later.

## Safety and comfort

- Avoid topics that may cause grief, such as a recent death, unless the person raises it.
- Keep objects in the twenty-questions round safe to name. Avoid anything sharp or small enough to swallow.
- Do not correct a story unless it affects safety. Listen first.
