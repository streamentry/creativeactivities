Many people want something enjoyable to do with an older parent or grandparent who lives with them, lives nearby or is visited once a week. This page is for that moment. The ideas are grouped by how much energy the person has and how much their hands, eyes or ears are doing the work. They are suggestions only. You know the person best.

## What do you need?

Most of these ideas use things already in the house: paper and pencils, a radio or phone with music, a basket of yarn, a tray of buttons, a pack of cards, a few flowers, or a window with birds outside. Each linked card lists its own materials. Read the list before you start, so you are not searching for something halfway through.

## Activities for a low-energy day

Some days a person has very little energy. These ideas take ten to twenty minutes and can be done sitting down.

1. **Listen to music together.** Choose two or three songs from their younger years, then let them choose the next one. [Draw to music](/activity/draw-to-music/) adds a pencil if they want to make marks while they listen.
2. **Look through a photo album.** Ask, "Tell me about this one," rather than "Who is this?" The [photo album card](/activity/look-through-a-photo-album/) has more prompts.
3. **Wind a ball of yarn.** Hold the skein loosely between their hands while you wind. It is a simple, rhythmic task with an obvious end. See [wind a ball of yarn](/activity/wind-a-ball-of-yarn/).
4. **Watch birds from a window.** Sit where the light is good and point out what you see. See [watch birds from a window](/activity/watch-birds-from-a-window/).
5. **Tap a rhythm together.** Take turns tapping on the table. Keep it short. [Tap a rhythm together](/activity/tap-a-rhythm-together/) has the steps.

For someone who enjoys touch, [give a hand massage](/activity/give-a-hand-massage/) can be a calm ending. Ask first, and stop as soon as they pull their hand away.

## Activities for a good-energy day

On a good day you can offer something with more doing in it.

- **Arrange flowers together.** Cut the stems, choose a vase and place each bloom in turn. See [arrange flowers together](/activity/arrange-flowers-together/).
- **Paint with watercolors.** Use large paper and a big brush, and let the colors spread. See [paint with watercolors](/activity/paint-with-watercolors/).
- **Fold towels together.** It is useful work, and it has a clear finish. See [fold towels together](/activity/fold-towels-together/).
- **Make a memory collage** from photos and paper, with one picture at a time. See [make a memory collage](/activity/make-a-memory-collage/).
- **Sort buttons by color** into shallow dishes, for someone who likes order and small tasks. See [sort buttons by color](/activity/sort-buttons-by-color/).
- **Make a playlist together** on a phone or speaker, adding one song at a time. See [make a playlist together](/activity/make-a-playlist-together/).

## Adjusting for grip, sight, hearing and mobility

Think about what is getting in the way before you change the activity.

**Grip.** Use thick markers, jumbo crayons, a large brush with a fat handle, or foam tape wrapped around thin handles. Tape the paper down so it does not slide. Pre-cut shapes can take the place of scissors.

**Sight.** Use high contrast, such as black marker on white paper. Use large print and good light from the side rather than from behind. A plain tablecloth makes small pieces easier to see.

**Hearing.** Sit face to face, speak slowly and turn off the television or radio if it competes with your voice.

**Mobility.** Bring the activity to the chair or the bed rather than asking them to move. Keep water and tissues within reach, and check that the table height suits their chair.

**Speech.** Offer choices with pictures or gestures. A nod or a point counts as taking part. You can also do the activity beside them and talk about what you are using.

## When a parent is living with dementia

Several of these ideas are often used with people living with dementia. A few changes help most: offer one step at a time, let them choose from two options, use familiar objects and music, and expect the activity to take longer or change partway through. If a story goes somewhere unexpected, enjoy it and ask about the feeling: "That sounds like it mattered to you."

The Alzheimer's Association [activities page](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) suggests keeping the person's skills in mind, sticking with activities they have always enjoyed and adjusting them as needed. The NHS [activities page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) says a shared activity can make both people happier and help them enjoy time together.

For more ideas, see [activities for people with dementia](/activities/ideas/) and the [dementia activities hub](/dementia-activities/).

## Safety notes

- Keep small items, such as buttons, beads, seeds and pins, in trays or dishes you can cover. Clear them away when you stop.
- Keep scissors and knives out of reach unless you are seated beside the person and the task needs them.
- Use washable, non-toxic materials. Check for allergies before using flowers, herbs or scented items.
- Keep water cups away from the paint, and do not let a jar of water be mistaken for a drink.
- If the person becomes upset, stop, offer a cup of tea and change the activity. The [guide on when someone says no](/caregiving/when-they-say-no/) has more on this.

## If it is not working

Change one thing: the activity, the time of day, the table, the length or the amount of choice. If the person still says no, do not press. Sitting in the room together with the radio on counts as time together. Try again another day. Many people come back to an activity they turned down the week before.

## For you too

You are part of the activity. A short session you both enjoy is better than a long one that leaves you both tired. Keep the goal small: one song, one photo, one row of folded towels. It is fine to end there.
