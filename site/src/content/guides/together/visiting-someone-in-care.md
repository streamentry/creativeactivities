Visiting someone you love in a care home, a hospital or a hospice can bring up many feelings. You may wonder what to say, what to bring and whether the visit still matters if the person cannot share much. It does. This page is practical. It covers planning the visit, what to bring, what to do together, what to share with staff, and how to leave. It also explains the extra care needed in a hospital and near the end of life.

## Before the visit

A little planning makes the visit easier for both of you.

- **Pick a good time.** Some people are more alert in the morning or around midday. Plan for the time when the person is usually most rested and comfortable.
- **Learn the schedule.** The Family Caregiver Alliance suggests visiting during free time rather than activity time, so the person is not in the middle of a group session or a meal.
- **Check with staff.** Ask how the person has been today, and check any rules about food, pets, outings and the length of visits.
- **Plan a few topics.** The person may not have news to share, so bring your own: family updates, the weather, a ball game or a funny story.
- **Set your expectations.** Some visits will be warm and chatty. Others will be quiet or hard. Both are normal.

## What should you bring?

Bring things to share, not things that test.

- **A photo album** with large, clear prints. Label the photos on the back for staff.
- **A playlist** of favorite songs on a phone, with simple headphones or a small speaker.
- **Something soft to hold,** such as a familiar blanket, scarf or sweater, if the setting allows it.
- **Hand lotion** for a gentle hand massage, if the person enjoys touch.
- **A magazine or picture book** about an old interest, such as gardening, cars, birds or cooking.
- **A snack or a bunch of flowers,** but only after you have checked with staff.
- **A tablet** for looking at family photos or playing a gentle game. [Picture pairs](/games/picture-pairs/) can be played with pictures left visible and no timer, and [photo pairs](/games/photo-pairs/) turns photos you choose into a matching game.

## What can you do together?

Doing something together takes the pressure off conversation.

1. **Look at photos.** Point out what you see: "That hat is lovely." Let the person lead. Avoid "Who is this?"
2. **Play music and sing.** A familiar song can make a shared moment, even when talking is hard.
3. **Take a short walk.** Walk around the building or into the garden if there is one. A wheelchair stroll counts.
4. **Give a hand massage,** if the person has agreed. Use a little lotion and slow strokes, and stop if they pull away.
5. **Help with grooming** if they enjoy it, such as brushing hair or painting nails. Ask first.
6. **Play a simple game,** such as cards, dominoes or a picture game on a tablet.
7. **Watch something together,** such as a comedy or an old film, and sit close.
8. **Read aloud,** a short poem, a favorite passage or a letter from someone else in the family.
9. **Sit quietly.** Holding hands or sitting side by side is a visit.

For more ideas that work at a bedside or in a chair, see [reminiscence](/activities/reminiscence/) and [activity ideas](/activities/ideas/).

## In a hospital

A hospital visit can disrupt routines and be hard to follow. Hospital staff's instructions come first.

- **Ask what to bring.** Glasses, hearing aids and familiar clothes are often welcome. Ask before you bring food, drink or medicine, and do not give any of them unless staff say it is safe.
- **Share a short note with staff.** With the person's agreement, write a brief note that covers their preferred name, how they communicate, what helps them understand and any comfort items that matter. Keep it short and private, and share it only with the staff who need it.
- **Let the person speak for themselves.** Ask staff to speak to them directly, use plain words and allow time for an answer.
- **Tell staff about changes.** If the person seems suddenly more confused, or unlike their usual self, tell the nurse or doctor straight away. Do not assume it is only dementia.
- **Plan the next visit with the team.** Ask who will be your main contact for updates, and when is the best time to come.

## Near the end of life

Visits in a hospice, or in the last days of life in any setting, may be quieter. The NHS [end of life care](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/end-of-life-care/) information explains that this care can happen at home, in hospital, in a care home or in a hospice, and that support is available for the people close to the person as well.

- Ask staff what the person would like, and whether they can still enjoy music, touch, photos or a familiar voice.
- Speak softly and say who you are, even if you think they know you.
- Keep the visit short if the person is tired. Sitting in the room together is enough.
- Grief may come up during or after the visit. You do not have to hold it alone. Ask staff about family support, or a bereavement service.

## Safety and comfort

- Wash your hands before and after the visit, and follow the signs on the ward.
- Ask before any touch, and stop if the person pulls away or looks uncomfortable.
- Keep sharp tools and water away from equipment, and do not share food unless staff say it is allowed.
- Do not put a person's health details on a social media post or a shared board.

## Leaving well

End while the person is still engaged, if you can. Say what is next: "I will see you on Thursday." Thank the staff, and take a few breaths before you drive or go home. Sometimes a visit feels full and sometimes it feels empty. Both are normal.

## If it is not working

Shorten the visit, change the activity or sit quietly. If the person is restless, lower the sound and move to a calmer spot. If they become upset, stop and say something gentle, such as "Let's just sit for a while." Staff can help if you are unsure.

## For you too

Visiting is hard work. You are allowed to leave early, to come back another day, and to ask for support. If you feel overwhelmed, the crisis support on this page is there for you.
