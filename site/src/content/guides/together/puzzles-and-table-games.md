Puzzles and table games suit mixed groups well. A jigsaw on the table lets people sit together without needing to talk, and the picture gives everyone something to comment on. A round of matching or a simple card game works for a grandparent, a teenager and a toddler sitting in the same room, if the rules are flexible. This page covers jigsaws, word puzzles, matching games and table games, and how to make each one enjoyable rather than tiring.

## Why these games work at a shared table

They give people something to do with their hands and eyes, and an easy place to start. They are also easy to pause and easy to finish later. The NHS [activities page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) notes that online games and puzzles are one way people stay engaged. The Alzheimer's Association [activities page](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) suggests focusing on enjoyment rather than achievement.

Puzzles and games are for enjoyment and company. They are not a treatment, and the goal is an absorbing hour together.

## How do you choose a jigsaw?

Go by what the person enjoys. Look at these four things.

- **Number of pieces.** Start with about half the number used last time, then go up if it is too easy.
- **Piece size.** Larger pieces are easier to see and pick up. Thick, sturdy pieces stay put.
- **Picture.** Choose adult subjects such as gardens, kitchens, boats, farm animals or a favorite hobby. Avoid cartoons and busy scenes. Distinct areas of color, such as a red barn on a green field, are easier than a plain sky.
- **Finish.** A matte finish reduces glare from lamps and windows.

A printed photograph from home can make a very good puzzle. Some print shops offer this service.

## How do you set up a jigsaw together?

1. **Pick a good spot** with light from the side and a chair with arms.
2. **Prop up the box lid** so you can both see the picture.
3. **Turn the pieces face up** before you begin.
4. **Find the edges** together, or do the border yourself and leave the middle for them.
5. **Group pieces by color** into a few shallow trays.
6. **Hand over one piece at a time** if the pile feels like too much: "I think this one is part of the barn."
7. **Talk about the picture,** not the progress: "That looks like the boat my dad had."
8. **Leave it out.** Come back over several days. There is no hurry.

## Word puzzles and word search

Word searches and simple crosswords with large print suit many people who enjoy words. Circle or highlight words in a bright color, and read the words aloud together. Choose a word list about a topic the person knows: fruit, flowers, cars, ships, towns. Skip timers. A word nobody finds can be left for later, or you can point to it together.

## Matching games and memory-style games

Classic memory games, where cards are turned face down and you must remember where each pair is, can be hard. They ask for something new to be held and recalled, which is often the first thing that feels difficult. The matching can still be enjoyable if you change how it works.

- **Leave the pictures face up.** Matching pictures you can see is a real game.
- **Start with two or three pairs.** Add more only if they want more.
- **Choose big, familiar, adult pictures,** such as a teacup, a sailboat or a sunflower.
- **Play as a team.** "Let's find the dogs" works better than "your turn."
- **Drop the score and the clock.** Nobody needs to win.

Try [picture pairs](/games/picture-pairs/) with pictures left visible, or [find the same](/games/find-the-same/) for a simpler choice of one picture against two or three. [Sort together](/games/sort-together/) asks you to put each picture where it belongs, such as the kitchen or the garden, which draws on knowledge the person already has.

## Table games for mixed groups

Many simple table games work well across ages. Try a card game such as a simple matching or snap-style game with no score kept, or a picture bingo where everyone calls out what they see. [Picture bingo](/games/picture-bingo/) uses large pictures and can be shared by a whole table. Dominoes can be played in pairs or with a team of two, with the rule that you help each other.

For every table game, agree these rules at the start:

1. Nobody keeps score unless everyone asks for it.
2. Players can ask for help, and a helper can point or pass a card.
3. Anyone may watch and leave the table at any time.
4. The game ends when people stop enjoying it, not when someone wins.

## Easier versions

- Use fewer pieces, fewer cards or fewer pairs.
- Use a tray with raised edges so pieces do not slide.
- Pre-sort pieces by color or by shape.
- Hand each person one card at a time.
- Play in a seated position, with the board at a comfortable height.
- Choose high-contrast pieces for low vision, and large print for word games.

## Safety notes

- Keep small pieces, such as jigsaw pieces, game tokens and dice, away from anyone under three and from anyone who may put objects in their mouth.
- Store pieces in a tray or box you can close. Count the pieces at the end of a session.
- Use games with no small magnetic parts or button batteries.
- If a player becomes upset, stop, change the game and offer a drink.

## If it is not working

Change the number of pieces, the picture or the rules, or simply sit together and sort the pieces. If the game feels like work, put it away. Leave a half-finished puzzle on a table or tray and come back to it later. Many people enjoy returning to a puzzle they started the week before.

## With someone living with dementia

For someone who used to enjoy puzzles, a familiar picture and a few large pieces often bring back the pleasure. Keep the picture visible, sit beside them rather than across, and hand over one piece at a time. Watch for signs that the task is too hard, such as turning away or pushing the pieces aside. Offer to do the next piece together, or switch to something simpler. Choose a moment when they are rested and not hungry or rushed.
