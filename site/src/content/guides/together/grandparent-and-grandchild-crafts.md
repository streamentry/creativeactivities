Grandparents and grandchildren often look for something to do together that is not a screen. A craft gives each person a job. The grandparent is treated as an adult with a role and an opinion, and the child is trusted with real work. This page sets out roles for each person, a short list of crafts that suit a range of ages and grip, and the safety steps for young children. For more pairings, see the [grandparent and grandchild activities hub](/grandparent-grandchild-activities/).

## Set the roles before you start

Say out loud who chooses, who holds, who writes and who tidies up. A child who has a job will stay with it longer. A grandparent who is asked for an opinion will feel useful rather than managed.

| Craft | Grandparent's part | Child's part |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Family tree of hands](/activity/make-a-family-tree-of-hands/) | Chooses the trunk and says each name | Traces hands in pencil and adds ages |
| [Photo postcard](/activity/make-a-photo-postcard/) | Says the message and who it is for | Picks the photo and writes the name |
| [Paper boat](/activity/fold-a-paper-boat/) | Shows each fold, slowly | Follows the folds, then sails the boats |
| [Paint a garden stone](/activity/paint-a-garden-stone/) | Chooses the colors and the pattern | Paints, with an adult nearby |
| [Weave paper strips](/activity/weave-paper-strips/) | Holds the frame and sets the pattern | Tears and threads the strips |
| [Draw repeating patterns](/activity/draw-repeating-patterns/) | Draws the first row | Continues the pattern |

Treat the grandparent as a grown-up with a role. Ask their opinion, and let them correct you. Their stories are the main part of the visit. The craft is the reason to sit together.

## Crafts for a mixed age table

Several cards on this site work well across generations. Choose one that fits the time, the room and the child's age.

- [Tear paper mosaic](/activity/tear-paper-mosaic/) uses torn paper and glue. The child tears, the grandparent places.
- [Make a greeting card](/activity/make-a-greeting-card/) can be sent to someone else in the family. Use pre-cut shapes for the grandparent, if that helps.
- [Make a paper chain garland](/activity/make-a-paper-chain-garland/) is good for a group. The child tears the strips and the grandparent loops and joins them.
- [Tap a rhythm together](/activity/tap-a-rhythm-together/) needs no materials at all, and is a good way to start or end a visit.

## Safety for young children

Keep the adult close and the materials simple.

- **Under three: no small parts.** Leave out beads, buttons, small stones, seeds, dried beans and anything that fits through a toilet paper tube. Several cards on this site list small parts, so check the materials before you start.
- **No balloons** and no small pieces of foam or plastic in the craft.
- **Washable, non-toxic materials** only, with an adult supervising any paint or glue.
- **No hot glue or hot tools.** Use a glue stick or a washable school glue.
- **Scissors** only with an adult, and only for older children who have used them before. Tearing paper is a good alternative.
- **No food** as a craft material. Keep edible items for eating, and away from the table where you are working.
- **Keep paint water in a cup,** not a basin, and clear it away when the child has finished.
- **Wash hands** after paint, glue or soil.

Do not photograph children for sharing, and do not post a child's name, age or school online without the parents' agreement. Keep the family tree, the recipe book and the songbook within the family.

## Adapting for a grandparent with limited grip or sight

- Use jumbo crayons, thick markers and large brushes with padded handles.
- Tape the paper to the table so it does not slide.
- Pre-cut shapes, or pre-tear the strips, so the grandparent can place them rather than cut them.
- Use high contrast, such as black marker on white paper, and large print on any labels.
- Sit side by side so the child and the grandparent can both see the work.

The [art and music guidance](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/art-music) from the Alzheimer's Association notes that art projects can give a sense of accomplishment and purpose. It also advises using safe materials and avoiding toxic substances and sharp tools. Those rules suit a mixed-age table well.

## If the grandparent has memory changes

Use simple, honest words before the visit. Say what is different and what stays the same: Grandpa may forget a name or tell the same story again, and he still enjoys company. Ask the child for a small part of the story, rather than a test. Invite them to ask, "What was it like when you were little?" Avoid asking whether the grandparent remembers something.

If the child asks a question you cannot answer, say so. "I do not know, but we can ask Grandma when she is ready." Honest answers help children feel safe.

## If it is not working

Shorten the craft, change the task, or stop. A child who has lost interest does not need to finish. Put the materials in a box and come back another day. If the grandparent is tired, make the child's part bigger and let the grandparent watch and comment.

## Tips for a good session

- Lay out all the materials before the child arrives.
- Choose a table with good light and a cloth to protect it.
- Start with the simplest step, so the child feels successful early.
- Finish with something to keep, show or give away.
- End with a snack and a walk, or a quiet story.
