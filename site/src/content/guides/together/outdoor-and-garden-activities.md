For many people the garden is a place they know well. Digging, watering and picking flowers use skills learned long ago, and being outside brings fresh air, light and birdsong. A garden can also be shared across ages: a grandparent can show a child how to water, and an adult can sit in the shade and name the flowers. This page covers what to try, how to make outdoor spaces easier to use, and how to stay safe.

## What to try in the garden

The best tasks are simple and have a clear start and finish. Pick ones that suit the person's energy and what they used to enjoy.

1. **Watering.** Use a small, light can or a gentle hose nozzle, and keep a few pots within reach. Watering is hard to get wrong.
2. **Planting seeds in pots.** Large seeds, such as beans, sunflowers or peas, are easier to handle. The Alzheimer's Foundation of America suggests planting seeds in small pots and watching them grow. See [plant seeds in a pot](/activity/plant-seeds-in-a-pot/).
3. **Deadheading.** Pinching off faded flowers by hand is gentle and repetitive. Choose plants without thorns.
4. **Picking herbs or vegetables.** Cherry tomatoes, beans and herbs are easy to pick and nice to use at lunch.
5. **Smelling and touching herbs.** Rosemary, mint and lavender have strong scents. Rub a leaf between your fingers and pass it over.
6. **Sweeping a path or patio,** if the surface is flat and dry and balance is steady.
7. **Filling a bird feeder,** then sitting together to see who comes.

## Indoor options

When going outside is not possible, bring the garden in.

- **A windowsill herb pot** of basil, parsley or chives.
- **Potting on a covered tray.** Cafeteria-style trays keep soil in one place, and small cups make it easy to move soil into a pot.
- **Flower arranging** with cut flowers and greens from the yard or a shop. See [arrange flowers together](/activity/arrange-flowers-together/).
- **Pressed flowers** from a bouquet, later glued onto cards. See [press leaves and flowers](/activity/press-leaves-and-flowers/).
- **Printing with leaves** on paper, using a little paint. See [print with leaves](/activity/print-with-leaves/).

## Getting out

- **A short walk** around the block, a park or a garden center, at a slow pace with a place to sit.
- **Sitting outside** on a porch or bench to listen to birds and feel the breeze. This counts as a whole activity.
- **Visiting a public garden** at a quiet time of day. Many gardens have areas with scented plants, textures and water. Ask ahead about seating and paths.
- **Feeding birds or watching ducks** at a pond, keeping a safe distance from the water's edge.

## How to make a garden easier to use

Bring the garden up to the person.

- **Raised beds or tall planters** at about waist height mean no kneeling or bending.
- **Containers on a table** suit someone who uses a wheelchair or prefers to sit.
- **Wide, level paths** with no loose stones or hoses across them lower the risk of trips.
- **Seats with arms and shade** along the way, so resting is always an option.
- **Tools with thick, soft handles.** Skip pruners, knives and anything sharp.

## Which plants should you avoid?

Choose plants that are safe to touch and, ideally, that do not irritate skin. The Alzheimer's Foundation of America advises using only plants that are not known to cause skin irritation, checking for allergies, and being mindful of anyone who might mistake soil for food.

- Do not assume a plant is safe to eat. Many common garden plants and berries are toxic if swallowed.
- Keep bulbs, seeds, pellets and soil treated with products out of reach, especially for young children and for anyone who puts things in their mouth.
- Check allergies before planting or cutting flowers, herbs and scented leaves.

## Heat, sun and wandering

- Choose cool times of day, and keep sessions short.
- Offer water often, and keep a shady spot for rest. A hat helps.
- Never leave anyone alone in strong sun.
- If the person has wandered before, keep the garden secure with a latched gate and stay in sight. Ask their care team for advice.

## Easier versions

- **Seated potting.** Put a tray on a table and pot up a small plant together.
- **One-hand watering.** Use a light can with a short spout, or water a pot the person can lift.
- **Picking from a bowl.** Bring herbs or flowers to the chair and let them pick, sort or smell.
- **Watching the garden.** Sit by a window or in the shade, and name what you see together.

## Safety notes

- Keep tools, pots and sharp items out of reach when you are not using them.
- Wash hands after soil, and do not let anyone eat soil, seeds or plant material.
- Check the ground for trip hazards before starting.
- Stop if the person becomes tired, too warm or distressed.

## If it is not working

Change the place, the time of day or the task. A garden that is too much to manage can be reduced to a single pot or a bowl of herbs on the table. Sitting outside together is enough on some days.

## With someone living with dementia

A familiar garden can be very welcome. Keep tasks simple and familiar, and sit beside the person rather than across. Use trays and small cups to keep soil contained. Keep plants that are safe to touch and avoid anything the person might mistake for food. Expect them to need help starting a task, and let them stop when they are ready.
