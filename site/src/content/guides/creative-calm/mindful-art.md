Mindful art is a slow way of making marks. You pick one simple shape or subject and draw it a little at a time, looking closely between each line. Nothing has to look like anything. The point is the attention, the pace and the materials, not a finished picture. This is a creative activity, not therapy. If you or the person you care for has a health concern, talk with your own doctor or a licensed professional before you start anything new.

## Who is this for?

Mindful art suits adults who enjoy making things but feel unsure about drawing. It also suits people who like repetition, pattern and quiet, unhurried tasks. You can do it alone, or side by side with a friend, a partner or someone you care for. It works at a table, or in bed with a lap tray. These are suggestions, not a diagnosis. Some people will enjoy it, and others will not.

## What do you need?

- Plain paper, a half sheet of printer paper or a small sketch pad
- A fine pen, a soft pencil and an eraser
- A few colored pencils or fine markers
- One small object to draw, such as a cup, a leaf, a shell or a key
- A clipboard, if the table is uneven or you want to draw in bed

Household items are fine. Use what you have.

## How do you start a slow drawing?

1. Set the object on the table in good light, a short distance from you.
2. Choose one part of it, such as the rim of the cup or the outline of one leaf.
3. Draw very slowly, looking at the object more than at the paper. Let the line wander.
4. After a few lines, stop. Look at the drawing, then look at the object again.
5. On another part of the sheet, start a pattern: a row of small circles, a line of waves, or a grid of dots. Repeat the same shape, one after another.
6. Add color only if it is pleasant. Use one color at a time and let it fill one area.
7. Stop when you want to. Leave the page where it is and come back another day.

Contour drawing, where you trace an outline without lifting the pen and mostly do not look down, is a good first step. The [draw repeating patterns](/activity/draw-repeating-patterns/) and [sketch what is on the table](/activity/sketch-what-is-on-the-table/) cards have printable steps.

## What to try next

- **Repeating shapes.** Choose a single mark, such as a loop or a small triangle, and fill a strip of paper with it. Change one thing at a time, such as the size or the spacing.
- **Slow grids.** Draw a grid of squares and fill each square with a different simple mark. Nobody has to fill them in order.
- **Color by area.** Lay down one color in a loose shape, then another, and let the edges meet. This works well with [paint with watercolors](/activity/paint-with-watercolors/) too.
- **Drawing to sound.** Put on a piece of music and let each line follow it. The [draw to music](/activity/draw-to-music/) card explains how.
- **Texture rubbing.** Place paper over a leaf or a textured tile and rub with the side of a crayon. Look at the pattern that appears.

## Easier versions

- **Seated or in bed.** Rest the paper on a clipboard or a lap tray, and keep the pen within reach.
- **Easier to grip.** Use a thick marker or wrap a thin pencil in foam tape. Draw large shapes instead of small ones.
- **Low vision.** Use a dark marker on white paper, a larger object and good light. Describe the shape aloud as you draw it.
- **Fewer choices.** Offer one pen and one pattern, and leave out the color.
- **Shorter.** Draw one row of shapes and stop. Two minutes counts.
- **A start already on the page.** Begin with a simple outline already drawn, and add lines or color inside it.

## Safety notes

- Keep pen caps, pencil sharpeners and small erasers away from small children and from anyone who might put things in their mouth.
- Use washable markers and non-toxic paint, and read the label.
- If holding the tool causes pain or the grip is weak, put it down. Try a larger tool, a rest for the wrist, or another day.
- Work on a clean table with a surface that will not be marked by ink or water.

## If it isn't working

If the person is bored, tired or uninterested, switch to a simpler pattern or stop. If they say they cannot draw, try mark-making with no subject at all: lines, dots and loops, with no right or wrong. If the activity brings up something painful, stop, offer a warm drink and do something familiar. Stopping is a good outcome, and the page will keep.

The [coloring](/games/coloring/) game offers a related quiet task with ready-made outlines, and can be an easier place to start on a day when drawing feels like too much.

A 2019 WHO Regional Office for Europe scoping review looked at more than 3,000 studies and describes links between arts activity and health and well-being. Read it as background, not as proof that any one activity will help any one person.
