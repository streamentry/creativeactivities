Sensory activities use the senses of touch, smell, sound and sight. We leave taste out, so nothing in them is meant to be eaten. They are simple experiences built around one sense at a time, like feeling a soft scarf, smelling mint or listening to rain. This is a creative activity, not therapy. A few safety checks make them relaxing for everyone. If you or the person you care for has a health concern, such as swallowing trouble, a skin condition or an allergy, talk with your own doctor or a licensed professional first.

## Who is this for?

Sensory activities suit adults at many stages of life. They work well for someone who likes to handle things, for someone who has less energy and wants a gentle task, and for someone who spends much of the day in a chair or bed. They can also be shared between generations, such as a child and an adult handling the same basket of fabric. These are suggestions, not a diagnosis.

## What do you need?

- A basket or box for the items, with a lid if you want to keep it tidy
- Large, safe items: soft fabric squares, a wooden spoon, a smooth stone, a sprig of herb
- Unscented or mild lotion, if skin is healthy and the person is not allergic
- A quiet room, with the television turned off
- A warm cloth, a cup of water and a chair for the person to sit in

## Sensory ideas, sense by sense

**Touch**

- **Hand massage.** Warm a little unscented lotion in your hands and stroke slowly from wrist to fingertips. The [give a hand massage](/activity/give-a-hand-massage/) card has steps.
- **Fabric basket.** Large squares of velvet, corduroy, fleece, satin and wool to stroke and fold. The [make a texture board](/activity/make-a-texture-board/) card turns the same idea into something to keep.
- **Folding and smoothing.** Clean washcloths or napkins to fold, stack and refold. The [fold towels together](/activity/fold-towels-together/) card is a steady task.
- **Warm cloth.** A warm, damp cloth for the hands on a cold day. Test the temperature on your own wrist first.

**Smell**

- **Fresh herbs.** Rub a mint, rosemary or basil leaf between your fingers, then hold it near the nose.
- **Kitchen smells.** Coffee brewing, bread toasting, or an orange being peeled nearby.
- **Dried lavender.** A small sachet, sewn shut. The [make a lavender sachet](/activity/make-a-lavender-sachet/) card shows how.

**Sound**

- **Favorite music.** Familiar songs at a gentle volume. See [music for calm and connection](/creative-calm/music-for-calm-and-connection/).
- **Nature sounds.** Rain, waves or birdsong, played softly.
- **A shaker.** The [make a sealed shaker](/activity/make-a-sealed-shaker/) card makes a safe one to play along with music.

**Sight**

- **Window watching.** A bird feeder outside the window, or the view of a street. The [watch birds from a window](/activity/watch-birds-from-a-window/) card has ideas.
- **Color and pattern.** Sort buttons or fabric scraps by color with the [sort buttons by color](/activity/sort-buttons-by-color/) card. Choose large buttons only.
- **Flowers.** The [arrange flowers together](/activity/arrange-flowers-together/) card is a gentle way to handle, look at and smell fresh flowers.

## How do you make a sensory box?

1. Choose a theme linked to the person's life: the beach, the garden, the kitchen, the workshop or sewing.
2. Pick five or six large, safe items, such as a big seashell, a wooden spoon, a soft scarf, a sealed lavender sachet or a smooth wooden block.
3. Check every item for small parts, sharp edges, loose threads and anything breakable.
4. Bring out one item at a time. Hand it over, describe it, and let the person explore.
5. Put the box away afterward, and check the items again before the next use.

## Doing this with an older adult or someone living with dementia

Sensory activities matter most in the later stages of dementia. The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/stages-behaviors/late-stage) notes that at this point the world is experienced mainly through the senses, and that you can express care through touch, sound, sight, taste and smell. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) suggests that later-stage activities often focus on the senses, such as playing music, handling objects and hand massage.

Approach from the front, say the person's name, and ask or tell them before you touch: "I have some lotion. Would you like a hand rub?" Offer one thing at a time. Watch for a softer face, relaxed hands or a smile. Pulling away, frowning or restlessness mean stop. Keep the session to about five calm minutes.

## Safety notes

Sensory activities are gentle, but some risks matter, especially for someone who may put things in their mouth or who has a swallowing problem.

| Risk | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Choking | Use only items too large to swallow. No buttons, beads, coins or small toys loose in a box. |
| Things that look like food | Lock up or put away lotions, soaps, shampoos and perfume after use, since they can look or smell like food. Remove artificial fruit. |
| Allergies | Check for allergies to nuts, latex, wool, plants or fragrances before you start. |
| Strong scents | Avoid strong perfumes and essential oils, especially with asthma or other breathing problems. Mild, natural smells are safer. |
| Skin | Check for sore, broken or fragile skin before a massage. Use gentle pressure and stop if they wince. |
| Temperature | Test warm cloths and drinks on your own wrist first. |
| Sharp or toxic items | No sharp tools, glass or toxic craft supplies. |

## Does aromatherapy help?

Probably not in any measurable way. A [Cochrane review of aromatherapy for dementia](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003150_aromatherapy-dementia) found 13 studies with 708 people, all living in care homes. The studies were small, their results did not agree, and it found no evidence of benefit on the outcomes it looked at. If the person enjoys a mild, familiar smell, such as lavender from the garden, that is a fine reason to share it. It is not a treatment.

## A low-sensory option

Some people find a lot of input tiring. A low-sensory version uses one object, soft light, a warm blanket and no scent. Sit together and let the light move across the wall, or hold one smooth stone. Keep voices low and offer a pause whenever you like. If the person turns away or closes their eyes, that is a complete session.

## If it isn't working

If the person seems uneasy, stop, put the item away and sit quietly. Try a different sense another day, or a different time of day. Some people prefer a morning session, and others prefer one when the house is quiet in the evening. Stopping early is a good outcome.

The [calm breathing](/games/calm-breathing/) pause is a gentle way to end a session, with one slow breath each and no pressure to do more.
