Caring for someone is hard, and looking after yourself is part of the job. This page offers short creative breaks, and points to real support when you need more than a break. These are creative activities, not therapy. If you or the person you care for has a health concern, talk with your own doctor or a licensed professional.

## What does a five-minute creative break look like?

Small breaks count. None of these fix exhaustion, but they can get you through a hard afternoon.

- **Press a leaf.** Put one leaf between the pages of a heavy book and come back to it later. The [press leaves and flowers](/activity/press-leaves-and-flowers/) card has steps.
- **Sketch the view.** Draw one thing from the window, or on the table, in pencil. Nobody needs to see it.
- **Draw a pattern.** Repeat one simple shape across a strip of paper. The [draw repeating patterns](/activity/draw-repeating-patterns/) card gives a clear next step each time.
- **Play one song you love.** Close your eyes, or tap along quietly. The [make a playlist together](/activity/make-a-playlist-together/) card can help you build one.
- **Write a short card.** A few lines to a friend, with a simple drawing. The [make a greeting card](/activity/make-a-greeting-card/) card is a quick way to do it.

## What about a longer break?

Set aside an hour once or twice a week for something with no goal. Paint a stone, arrange flowers in a jar, or shape a small pot from clay. The [paint a garden stone](/activity/paint-a-garden-stone/), [arrange flowers together](/activity/arrange-flowers-together/) and [shape a pinch pot](/activity/shape-a-pinch-pot/) cards all work for this. You can make something alone, or with the person you care for if they enjoy it.

If you cannot leave the person's side, a creative break can still be shared. The [sort buttons by color](/activity/sort-buttons-by-color/) card or a simple pattern on a sheet of paper can be done side by side.

## Easier breaks

- **Lying down.** Five minutes with your eyes closed and a favorite song in the background.
- **Fewer choices.** Pick one colored pencil and one sheet of paper. That is enough.
- **Low energy.** Sit by a window and count the birds, or hold a smooth stone.
- **Low vision.** Use bold markers and large paper, and describe what you are doing aloud.

## Where can you get respite care?

Respite care is short-term care for the person you look after, so you can rest. The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/respite-care) describes three common kinds:

| Type | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| In-home help | A friend, relative, volunteer or paid aide comes to the home |
| Adult day center | Planned activities in a group, often with transport and meals |
| Overnight or short stay | A long-term care community looks after the person for a night or longer |

Respite from family, friends or volunteers may cost nothing. Paid services usually charge by the hour, the day or the stay. Ask the Helpline about funding and options in your area, before you need them. Try a provider in a calm moment, so you know them when a sudden break is needed.

## Where can you find support?

- **Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline: 1-800-272-3900.** This US line is free, confidential and staffed around the clock. Help is available in many languages through bilingual staff or an interpreter service. You can talk through caregiver stress, respite options and local programs. Staff do not give medical advice.
- **Eldercare Locator: 1-800-677-1116 or eldercare.acl.gov.** A public service in the US that connects you to local services for older adults and their families.
- **Alzheimer's Association support groups.** Groups for caregivers, in person and online, are led by trained facilitators. Ask the Helpline which ones are near you.
- **Carers UK.** Information and advice for carers in the UK, including help on the practical side of caring.
- **Samaritans: 116 123 (UK and Ireland).** Free, and open 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, for anyone who needs to talk.
- **Find A Helpline.** Lists free helplines in many countries, when you do not know which service to call.

## How do you ask family and friends for help?

Be specific. Many people want to help but do not know what to offer.

- **Keep a list** of tasks others could take on, and let people choose one.
- **Start small.** A single afternoon, or one phone call a week, is a good first ask.
- **Match the task to the person.** The cousin who cooks can bring dinner on Thursdays. A neighbor who is good with forms can help with paperwork.
- **Text or email** if asking face to face feels hard.
- **Expect some no.** Try not to take it personally, and ask someone else.

## When should you get urgent help?

If you feel you cannot keep going, if you feel hopeless, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, reach out now. In the US, call or text 988 to reach the [988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/), free and available 24 hours a day. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans on 116 123. Elsewhere, [Find A Helpline](https://findahelpline.com/) lists free services by country. If anyone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

Talk with your doctor if signs of strain are regular, if you feel low for weeks, or if you are struggling to cope. Tell them you are a carer. They can help you plan support for both of you.

## Safety notes

- Use scissors and craft tools with care, and keep them away from the person you care for.
- Avoid fresh flowers, leaves and dried herbs if anyone has allergies or breathing problems. Check plants before you handle them.
- Clay and paint are not for eating. Wash hands after use.
- Take breaks from the break, too. A creative hour should never become another job.

## If it isn't working

If a creative break feels like one more task, try something smaller or simply sit. If the same stress keeps returning, ask for help with the caring itself, not only with the break. A support group, a respite visit or a conversation with your doctor can make more difference than any activity. Rest is not a reward you earn. It is part of keeping going.
