Music asks for nothing. There are no right answers and no steps to follow. A familiar song can bring a smile, a tapping foot or a line sung along, even on a quiet day. This is a creative activity, not therapy. If you or the person you care for has a health concern, talk with your own doctor or a licensed professional before you rely on music for anything more than enjoyment.

## Who is this for?

Music suits people of any age who enjoy listening, humming, singing or moving to a beat. It suits a grandparent and a grandchild sharing a song, a parent and an adult child looking through old records, and adults who are tired but want company. It also suits someone who is newly unwell and wants a gentle thing to share. These are suggestions. Music is not a replacement for care.

## What do you need?

- A speaker or a phone with a good battery, and a charger nearby
- A short list of three or four pieces, with one calm and one lively
- Paper and a pencil, if you want to write the playlist together
- Optional: a small hand drum, a shaker, or two wooden spoons

## How do you build a playlist from someone's life?

The most useful thing you can make is a playlist of music that means something to this person. Ask them, or ask the people who knew them when they were young.

- **Their teens and twenties.** What was on the radio, at dances and in films when they were young?
- **Big moments.** The music at a wedding, a first dance or a favorite family trip.
- **Faith and culture.** Hymns, gospel, sacred music, or songs in their first language.
- **Work and service.** Marching bands, work songs, or music tied to a place or a time.
- **What family remembers.** Ask siblings, children and old friends what they always played or sang.

If you are not sure where to start, try a few minutes from different styles and watch the response. Keep what brings a smile or a tapping foot. Drop what brings a frown. Keep two short lists: one calm for settling, and one lively for singing or dancing along.

## Activities to try together

1. **Listen together.** Sit side by side with nothing else going on. Listening is a complete activity.
2. **Sing along.** Start singing yourself. Humming or mouthing the words counts.
3. **Tap the beat.** Pat knees, tap the table or use a shaker. The [tap a rhythm together](/activity/tap-a-rhythm-together/) card has more ideas.
4. **Sway or dance.** Hold hands and sway in the kitchen, or move arms together while seated.
5. **Talk about the music.** Invite, do not test. Try "Tell me about the dances at that hall" rather than "Do you remember this song?"
6. **Make a personal songbook.** Write out the words of favorite songs in large print, with space to add notes. The [make a personal songbook](/activity/make-a-personal-songbook/) card has steps.
7. **Make a playlist together.** Take turns choosing songs. The [make a playlist together](/activity/make-a-playlist-together/) card gives an order to follow.
8. **Draw to the music.** Let lines and colors follow a piece as it plays. The [draw to music](/activity/draw-to-music/) card shows how.

## A short shared session

Five to twenty minutes is plenty. Turn off the television and close the door. Play one piece at a gentle volume. Sit where you can make eye contact, and let the person choose the next song if they want to. End with a quiet song, turn the music off, and sit together for a moment. Ending with silence is part of the activity.

## For an older adult or someone living with dementia

Music is one of the easiest things to share. Keep the choices simple, and let the person decide. The Alzheimer's Association suggests choosing music that is familiar and enjoyable, and a source that is not interrupted by commercials, which can cause confusion. Use music to set the mood you want: a tranquil piece for calm, and a well-loved tune for a lift.

Later in the course of dementia, the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities/) notes that activities often need to focus on the senses, and that playing music can be a good choice. Sit close, play the most loved songs very softly, and hold a hand or sway together. For more ideas that suit later stages, see [late-stage activities](/activities/late-stage/).

## Easier versions

- **Seated or in bed.** Use a speaker close by, with one song at a time.
- **Headphones or a speaker.** Some people enjoy headphones, which block noise. Others find them confusing. Try a speaker first so you can listen together.
- **Low vision.** Print the song titles in large type and read them aloud.
- **Hearing aids.** Make sure they are in and working before you start, and keep the volume comfortable.
- **Fewer choices.** Offer one song, or a single short playlist of three pieces.

## Safety notes

- Keep the volume comfortable. If the music is loud enough to make talking hard, turn it down.
- Some songs bring up grief, loss or painful memories. If that happens, comfort the person, change the song, and do not press them to explain.
- Do not copy song lyrics onto cards or into shared documents unless you have the right to do so. Listening and singing together are fine.
- If you use a shaker or drum, choose one with no small parts that could be swallowed.

## If it isn't working

If the person seems agitated, bored or unwell, stop the music and check for simple causes: pain, thirst, hunger, a full bladder, tiredness or a noisy room. Try a calmer piece, or simply sit together in quiet. Five minutes of music is a good session, and so is turning it off.

## What does the research say?

The evidence is limited. A [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003477_does-music-based-therapy-help-people-dementia) of structured music-based sessions included 30 studies with 1,720 people with dementia in 15 countries, mostly in nursing homes. Its results differ by outcome, and it describes the certainty of the evidence as limited for several of them. That review is about structured sessions led by a trained person. It is not a guide to listening at home.

We share music as a pleasant, low-pressure way to spend time together. It is not a treatment, and it does not replace care from a doctor or a licensed professional.
