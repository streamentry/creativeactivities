Toddlers are good at making art. They make marks, pour water, peel stickers and watch what happens next. None of this needs a finished result, and most of it can be done with a few simple materials and an adult close by. This page is for parents, grandparents and carers of toddlers, roughly 1 to 3 years old. It covers three ideas: big crayons, painting with water, and sticker play.

These are ideas to try, not milestones to reach. Every child is different, and a toddler who only wants to hold the crayon, or who wants to pour the water and not paint with it, is doing the activity well.

## Who these ideas suit

These activities suit toddlers who enjoy holding things, moving their arms and watching what changes. They are best for children aged about 1 to 3, with an adult sitting beside them. Some toddlers enjoy one idea and lose interest in the others, and that is normal. Offer one idea at a time, and keep the session short.

## Safety first

Read this section before you start any activity.

- **Stay within arm's reach.** Toddlers can be quick. Keep one hand near the child for every activity, and never leave a child alone with water.
- **Watch for choking.** Toddlers put things in their mouths. The Nemours KidsHealth choking guide lists deflated balloons, coins, arts and crafts supplies and small toy parts among common household choking hazards. As a simple test, anything that fits through a toilet roll tube is too small for a child under 3.
- **Leave out the small parts.** For under-3s, do not use beads, buttons, dried beans, small craft pieces, small stickers or any loose cap.
- **No balloons.** Do not offer balloons to a child under 3, whether latex or foil. Pieces of a burst balloon are a choking hazard.
- **Choose non-toxic, washable materials.** Look for a clear non-toxic label. The Art and Creative Materials Institute uses an Approved Product seal on art materials that have been reviewed, and a Cautionary Labeling seal where a warning applies. Read any label before the first use.
- **Check for allergies.** Ask the family before you use anything that contains food, such as a food-based dough. Some families avoid food dyes, so use washable paint or non-food color instead of food coloring.
- **Keep materials out of the mouth.** Remove crayon pieces, sticker backing and paper scraps as soon as they are no longer in use.

## Big crayons on big paper

Jumbo crayons are easy to hold and easy to make marks with, which is why they suit toddlers so well.

**What you need:** two or three jumbo, non-toxic, washable crayons; a large sheet of paper or the back of a roll of wallpaper; masking tape; a tray or wide space on the floor; wet wipes for the hands.

**Steps:**

1. Tape the paper flat to the table or floor so it does not slide.
2. Sit beside your child. Make a long line on the paper, and say what you are doing: "Down, and across."
3. Give the child one crayon. Let them make marks however they like.
4. If they copy you, carry on. If they put the crayon in their mouth, take it gently and offer a different one.
5. Name the colors as they go: "That is red." Point to a mark and say, "That's a big line."
6. Stop when your child stops. Wipe hands together and leave the paper out to look at later.

**Easier version:** Put the paper on a tray with raised sides. Place the crayon in the child's hand and wrap your hand over theirs for the first few strokes, then let go.

**Calmer version:** Use one color only. Sit on the floor with the paper on a cushion, and make quiet marks while the child watches.

## Painting with water

Painting with water is a good no-mess choice. The marks appear, change and fade, and nothing needs to be cleaned up with a cloth.

**What you need:** a shallow bucket or bowl of water; a large soft brush or a sponge with a thick handle; a wall, fence, paving or a large sheet of card that you can wet; a towel for the floor.

**Steps:**

1. Choose a flat, outdoor surface, such as paving or a garden wall, or use card indoors on a tray.
2. Fill the bowl about a third of the way. Keep it in front of you, within arm's reach.
3. Dip the brush or sponge, and show the child how to press it on the surface. Let them do it their own way.
4. Watch the marks dry and fade. Ask, "Where did the water go?" and let the child answer in any way they like.
5. Refill the brush when it is dry, and offer a second brush or sponge if the child wants a turn with another tool.
6. When the child is ready to stop, tip the water out and wipe the brush.

**Easier version:** Use a sponge with a thick handle, which is easier to grip than a thin brush. Dip it for the child and place it on the surface if needed.

**Calmer version:** Let the child pour water from a small cup onto a tray while you hold the bowl steady. Pouring is a full activity on its own.

## Sticker play

Stickers can be a good way to make pictures and to practice picking things up and putting them down. For toddlers, though, stickers need care.

**What you need:** a few large, non-toxic stickers that peel off easily; a sheet of plain paper or a window; a tray for the sheet of stickers.

**Steps:**

1. Peel one sticker from the sheet, and give it to the child.
2. Help them press it on the paper, or on the window at the child's height.
3. Name the shapes: "Round," or "A star."
4. Stick a few more, and let the child choose where each one goes.
5. Take the sticker sheet away when the activity is over, and put it out of reach.

**Easier version:** Peel the sticker in advance, and place it on the paper so the child can press it. This removes the fiddly part.

**Calmer version:** Offer only three stickers. A short activity, with a clear end, can feel calmer than a full sheet of choices.

Do not use small sticker sheets with toddlers. Stickers are small parts, and the backing paper is a risk if it is chewed.

## Doing it with a grandparent

A grandparent is often a good partner for this kind of play. They can sit alongside the child, paint their own sheet of paper with the same water brush, and let the child copy them. A grandparent may also have time to be quiet, which many toddlers enjoy. For more ideas for grandparents and grandchildren to try together, see the [together ideas](/together/) section.

Some grandparents worry about mess or about choking. Show them this page before you begin, and agree who will watch the child at each step.

## If it isn't working

- **The child puts everything in their mouth.** Stop, and offer a large, safe object to hold instead. Try the activity again another day, once the child has moved on from mouthing.
- **The child throws the crayons or brush.** This is common. Pick up the materials, and offer one at a time. End the session if throwing continues.
- **The child cries or walks away.** Let them go. Offer the activity again later in the day or another day, or sit nearby and try the water on the wall yourself.
- **The child is interested for only a minute.** That is a good session. Name what they did and finish.

If you are unsure whether a material is safe for your child, check the label, and check with your child's own clinician about any health question.
