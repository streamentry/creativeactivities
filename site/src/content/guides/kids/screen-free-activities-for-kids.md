Sometimes you have five minutes, sometimes an afternoon, and no plan. This page gives screen-free ideas sorted by how much time you have. Most need only paper, a pencil or a few things from around the house. A few need a walk outside.

This guide is for parents, grandparents and carers of children from about 3 upward. Younger children can join in with an adult's help, and the adult should be close by for every idea. Each idea says what you need and what to do.

## Who this suits

These ideas suit children who enjoy games, making things, moving and talking. They are also useful when a screen is the default and you would like another option. Screen-free time does not have to be a replacement for anything. It can simply be something else to do, and you can join in.

## Safety first

- **Adult nearby.** For children under 5, stay within reach for any activity with small items, water, scissors or outdoor play.
- **Small parts.** Under-3s should not have small items such as buttons, beads, coins, bottle caps or small toy parts. The Nemours KidsHealth choking guide lists these among common choking hazards.
- **Outdoors.** Check the weather, wear sun protection and keep to paths near water. Check children for ticks after a walk in long grass.
- **Plants.** Never let a child taste a plant or berry on a walk. Look, touch and sort, but do not eat.
- **Scissors and glue.** Use blunt scissors for children under about 6, and non-toxic glue.

## Five minutes

Use these when you have a short gap, such as before dinner or while waiting for a bus.

- **I spy something round.** Take turns to name something you can see that is a particular shape or color. Keep it going for as long as it is fun.
- **Clap it back.** Clap a short pattern, such as clap, clap, stamp. The child copies it. Then let them make a pattern for you. Use this one for children of any age, from a toddler clapping along to an older child making a longer pattern.
- **Move like the weather.** Call out weather words, such as wind, rain or sun, and let the child move in that way. Change the word every few seconds.
- **Count the steps.** Walk up the stairs together, counting each step out loud. Count back down.

## Twenty minutes

These need a little more setup, such as a sheet of paper or a bowl of water.

- **Paper-plate faces.** Give the child a paper plate and a few colored pens or crayons. Let them draw a face, and talk about the expressions. Use a pencil if you have no pens.
- **Tear-paper pictures.** Tear colored paper into pieces, and let the child glue them onto a sheet. There is no right picture. [Tear paper mosaic](/activity/tear-paper-mosaic/) has a full set of steps.
- **Bowl of warm and cool water.** Fill two bowls, one warm and one cool, checked on your wrist. Let the child pour between them and talk about what they feel. Keep an adult within arm's reach.
- **Draw to music.** Play a song with a clear beat, and let the child draw while they listen. The [draw to music](/activity/draw-to-music/) card explains how.

## One hour

Longer ideas give a child time to plan, make and change things.

- **Cardboard-box theatre.** Use a large cardboard box with a window cut in one side. Paint or color the inside, and make puppets from paper or socks. Put on a short show with a grown-up as the audience.
- **Paper-bag puppets.** For children 3 and up, decorate paper bags with crayons and glue on eyes and hair. Make a short story with the puppets. Keep scissors and glue with an adult.
- **Walk and collect.** Go for a walk and collect leaves, stones or pine cones, using a small bag. At home, sort the finds by color, size or shape. Never let a child taste anything collected outdoors.
- **Make a playlist together.** Choose five songs each, and let the child explain why they chose them. This works well for older children, who can write the list.

## A whole afternoon

For a rainy day or a long, slow weekend.

- **A den.** Drape sheets over a table or between two chairs, and make a space inside with cushions and books. Keep the structure low and stable, and keep cords and small objects out of reach of under-3s.
- **Indoor treasure hunt.** Make a list of pictures or words, such as a red sock, a spoon or a book with animals. Hide the items, or let the child find things around the house. Stop when they are tired.
- **Cooking without a recipe.** For children aged 5 and up, with an adult, make a simple snack with a few ingredients the family can eat. Check everyone's allergies first. Let the child choose the order of the steps.
- **Garden or balcony planting.** Plant a few seeds in a pot, and water them each day. Check the label on the packet, and wash hands after handling soil.

## Easier and calmer versions

- **Easier:** Use fewer steps and fewer materials. Do the first part yourself, and let the child finish it.
- **Calmer:** Choose a quiet activity, such as drawing or reading together, and turn off other sounds.
- **Shorter:** Stop after the first step. Many children are happy with a few minutes.
- **Seated:** Choose a table-based activity, and offer a cushion or a lap tray.

## Doing it with a grandparent

Grandparents can share many of these ideas. A grandparent can read out the "I spy" clues, or sit beside the child with paper and draw their own face while the child draws theirs. A grandparent who lives far away can join in by phone or video call, using the same paper and pens. Set the same activity at both ends of the call, and show each other the finished pictures. For more ideas, see the [together ideas](/together/) section.

## If it isn't working

- **The child wants a screen.** Stay calm, and offer one screen-free choice. You do not need to argue. Say, "We can do this one first," and join in.
- **The child is bored by the first idea.** Offer a different one from the same time band. A short change can help.
- **The child does not want to join in.** Let them watch, or let them choose the next idea. Watching is part of play.
- **The idea takes too long.** Stop after the first part and finish another day.

If you want to plan screen time with your family, the American Academy of Pediatrics explains how to make a family media plan. For any question about a child's health, ask your child's own clinician.
