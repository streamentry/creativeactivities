Children are different. Some dislike the feel of wet paint on their fingers. Some are upset by a noisy room. Some want to press, stomp and tear hard. Some want to watch from the edge of the table and then join in at their own pace. This guide shows how to adapt simple crafts so the child can choose their own level, rather than fitting the activity to a label.

This guide is for parents, grandparents and carers of children from about 3 upward. The ideas describe the activity and the choices. They do not describe the child, and they do not diagnose anything.

## Start with what your child chooses

Before you start a craft, watch your child in play. Notice which materials they touch and avoid, and whether they like loud or quiet, fast or slow. Then offer two options and let them choose. You do not need to decide in advance what your child needs.

## Choose-your-level options

These options can be added to most crafts.

- **Texture.** Dry, wet or tools only. A spoon, a brush or a glove can make it easier to handle something a child does not want on their fingers.
- **Noise.** A quiet version, or a version where the child controls a shaker, a drum or a spoon on a tray.
- **Predictability.** Three pictures showing the steps, in order. Put them on the table where the child can see them, and point to each step as you do it.
- **Space.** Floor, table, standing or a cushion. Let the child choose where to sit.
- **Breaks.** Plan stopping points. Say, "We can stop after this one," and mean it.
- **Movement.** It is fine to rock, tap, walk around or stand while doing the craft.
- **Participation.** A child can watch, or hold the materials, instead of making the piece. Watching counts as taking part.

## Safety first

- **Adult nearby.** Stay close for scissors, glue, paint and any tool, and for any craft with small pieces.
- **Non-toxic materials.** Look for a clear non-toxic label on paint, markers and dough. The Art and Creative Materials Institute uses an Approved Product seal on reviewed art materials.
- **Small pieces.** Under-3s should not have small pieces, such as buttons, beads, bottle caps or small stickers. The Nemours KidsHealth choking guide lists arts and crafts supplies among common hazards.
- **Allergies.** Homemade dough usually contains wheat and salt. Wheat is one of the common food allergens listed by Food Allergy Research and Education. Ask the family first.
- **Food dyes.** Some families avoid food dyes. Use washable, non-food color.
- **Cords and latex.** Keep string and cord away from under-3s, and avoid latex balloons and latex gloves.

## Finger painting or tools-only painting

Some children enjoy painting with their fingers. Others find it uncomfortable, or prefer a brush.

**Choose your level:**

- **Fingers:** a thick, washable paint on a large sheet taped to a tray.
- **Tools only:** a large sponge, a brush or a roller. Let the child hold the tool with a glove or a spoon if they prefer.
- **Quiet:** paint on paper, not on a hard surface, so the sound is softer.
- **Watching:** the adult paints, and the child chooses colors by pointing.

**Steps:** Put the paint out on a tray. Let the child choose their level. Let them make marks with the tool or the fingers. Wipe hands with a damp cloth whenever the child asks.

## Collage with glue or tape

Many children like the feel of gluing paper, but glue can be sticky or cold. Tape is a good alternative.

**Choose your level:**

- **Glue:** a glue stick, which is less wet than liquid glue.
- **Tape:** clear, wide tape stuck onto the card, with the paper pieces pressed on by the child.
- **Dry:** a card with a pre-drawn outline and paper pieces already torn, so the child just places them.
- **Watching:** an adult glues and the child chooses where each piece goes.

**Steps:** Draw a simple shape on card. Tear or cut the paper, or let the child tear it. Place the pieces and glue or tape them down. Let the child choose when to stop.

## Play dough with tools or gloves

Play dough can be firm, soft, cool or warm, and children feel it in different ways. Use a commercial dough labelled non-toxic if the family prefers, and check for allergies first.

**Choose your level:**

- **Hands:** the child squeezes and rolls.
- **Tools:** a blunt roller, a plastic spoon or a cookie cutter.
- **Gloves:** thin disposable gloves, if the child prefers them. Check first for latex.
- **Strong input:** the child presses the dough with a fist or palm, or flattens it with a large roller. Many children enjoy this kind of firm pressure.

**Safety:** Play dough is not for eating or mouthing. Keep it out of reach of under-3s.

## Tearing and stomping

Some children enjoy strong, large movements. These crafts give that movement a clear purpose.

**Stomping paint:** Put a sheet of paper on the floor, with a towel around it. Pour a little washable paint into a shallow tray, and let the child step into it and then onto the paper. Keep a damp cloth and a bowl of water by the door for feet.

**Hard tearing:** Give the child a large sheet of thick card or newspaper, and let them tear it into as many pieces as they like. Sort the pieces afterwards.

**Rolling and pressing:** Let the child roll a large sheet of play dough flat with a rolling pin, or press a large sponge into a tray of paint.

## Quiet versions, and versions the child controls

Some children want less noise, and some want to make a sound they choose.

- **Quiet:** choose paper, not card, and soft tools. Keep the room calm, and let the child decide whether to talk or not.
- **Child-controlled sound:** make a shaker from a sealed plastic bottle with dry rice or dried pasta, for children aged 5 and up with an adult close by. Let the child shake it when they like. Use a strong seal, and never use a bottle that could be opened.


## Respectful language

Describe what is on offer, and let your child decide.

- Say: "Would you like dry paint or wet paint?" Not: "This is a good activity for children who hate mess."
- Say: "We can make it quiet, or you can shake the bottle." Not: "This is for children who are noisy."
- Say: "Watching is a good way to take part." Not: "Some children just sit and watch."

Avoid labels for children, activities and groups of people. You will not need to name a condition to choose a craft.

## Doing it with a grandparent

A grandparent who knows the child's preferences can share them with the other adults who care for the child. A grandparent can hold the tray while the child chooses the level, or offer a glove for a child who prefers one. A grandparent who likes quiet can sit and watch, and join in when the child invites them. For more ideas to do together, see the [together ideas](/together/) section.

## If it isn't working

- **The child is upset by the texture.** Stop. Offer a tool, a glove or a dry version, and let them choose again.
- **The child is upset by the noise.** Turn down the sound, move the activity to a quieter room, or remove the shaker.
- **The child does not want to join in.** Let them watch, hold the materials or take a break. Offer again another day.
- **The child wants more movement.** Offer one of the stomping or tearing crafts, and let them move while they work.
- **You are not sure what the child needs.** Keep a note of what they chose and what they avoided. Share it with your child's own clinician, who can answer questions about health and development.

Your child's own clinician is the right person to ask about health, development, or any question about a diagnosis or treatment. This guide only offers ideas for crafts.
