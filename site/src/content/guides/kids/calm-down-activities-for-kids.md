Some children like quiet, slow things to do at the end of a busy day, or when they simply want something gentle. This guide offers five ideas that need little more than paper, crayons, a little play dough and a quiet room. Each one is a choice. The child can accept it, change it or say no, and you stop when they stop.

This guide is for parents, grandparents and carers of children from about 3 upward. Younger children can join in with an adult's help, but keep the activities short and close by.

## Who this suits

These ideas suit children who enjoy coloring, drawing, tearing paper or squeezing dough, and children who like a predictable, quiet activity. They can suit a child who is tired, a child who has been playing noisily, or a child who wants to be near you without a lot of talk. You know your child best. Offer one idea at a time, and notice what they choose.

These ideas are not treatments, and they do not promise any particular result. They are ordinary ways to spend quiet time together.

## Ground rules

- **Offer, do not prescribe.** Say, "Would you like to color for a bit?" rather than "You need to calm down."
- **The child can say no.** If they decline, offer something else or leave it for another time.
- **Stop when the child stops.** Do not carry on because you planned a longer session.
- **Never use it as a punishment.** Quiet activities should be something the child can enjoy, not something they lose.
- **Adult nearby.** Stay within reach for any activity with water, play dough, straws or small pieces.
- **Non-toxic and washable.** Look for a clear non-toxic label on crayons, paint and dough. The Art and Creative Materials Institute uses an Approved Product seal on reviewed art materials.

## Coloring with quiet music

**What you need:** a few large sheets of paper, or a coloring page; crayons or colored pencils; a quiet song or a set of soft instrumental music played at a low volume.

**Steps:**

1. Choose a picture or a blank sheet. Let the child choose the colors.
2. Play a quiet piece of music at a low volume, or sit without music.
3. Color beside the child, and do your own picture if you like.
4. Ask one open question, such as "Which color is the music?" and wait.
5. Stop when the child stops, and put the crayons back in their box together.

**Easier version:** Use a large, simple outline with thick lines. Offer three crayons instead of a full box.

**Calmer version:** Use one color and a blank sheet. Let the child draw whatever they like, or fill the page slowly with one shape.

## Tearing paper slowly

**What you need:** a sheet of colored tissue paper or thin craft paper; a bowl or tray for the pieces; a sheet of card and a glue stick, if the child wants to make a picture.

**Steps:**

1. Tear the paper into long, slow strips, one at a time. Let the child set the pace.
2. Sort the strips into the bowl by color, or leave them mixed.
3. If the child wants to make a picture, glue the strips on the card in any pattern.
4. Stop when the child stops. The strips can be kept for another day.

**Easier version:** Pre-tear the paper into strips, and let the child glue them.

**Calmer version:** Tear one color only, and tear slowly with both hands.

## Drawing to music

**What you need:** paper; crayons or markers; a piece of music with a clear beat or a slow, soft melody.

**Steps:**

1. Play the music at a low volume.
2. Ask the child to draw while they listen. Draw yourself, or sit quietly.
3. Ask, "Does the music go up or down?" Let the child answer with a line.
4. Stop after one or two pieces of music, or when the child is ready.

For a full set of steps, see [Draw to music](/activity/draw-to-music/).

**Easier version:** Draw one line for each few seconds of music, and let the child fill in the gaps.

**Calmer version:** Play quieter music, or none at all, and draw by looking at the paper.

## Play dough

Play dough is a good quiet activity for many children, but it needs care. Homemade dough is usually made with flour, salt and water, and flour contains wheat. Wheat is one of the common food allergens listed by Food Allergy Research and Education. Ask the family before you make or use any dough, and buy a commercial dough labelled non-toxic if you prefer.

**What you need:** a small amount of play dough, on a tray or mat; a rolling pin or a blunt tool; a few safe shapes, such as a plastic cookie cutter.

**Steps:**

1. Put the dough on the tray. Show the child how to squeeze, roll and flatten it.
2. Let them press a shape into the dough, or make a ball.
3. Ask what shape they would like to make, and let them choose.
4. Put the dough away in an airtight container when the child has finished.

**Safety notes:** Play dough is not for eating or mouthing. Do not give it to a child under 3, who may put it in their mouth. Keep small shapes and tools out of reach of under-3s, and wash hands after play.

**Easier version:** Use a larger piece of dough and a single large cutter. Let the child press the dough with their palm.

**Calmer version:** Keep the dough in one color. Sit quietly and squeeze your own piece.

## Gentle bubble breathing

Blowing bubbles through a straw in a cup of water is a playful activity. It needs an adult close by at all times.

**What you need:** a clear cup, half-filled with water; a plastic straw; an adult to sit beside the child.

**Steps:**

1. Fill the cup with a small amount of water. Put the straw in the cup, with the end just under the surface.
2. Show the child how to blow gently through the straw, to make small bubbles.
3. Let them try a few times, then stop. Pause between tries.
4. Empty the cup when the activity is over, and keep the straw away from the child's face when it is not in use.

**Safety notes:** Stay with the child for the whole activity. Use only a short plastic straw with a cup of water. Do not let a child drink from the cup, and do not let a child blow with the straw in their mouth without supervision.

**Easier version:** Let an adult hold the cup while the child blows. Skip the straw and blow soft bubbles onto a plate.

**Calmer version:** Offer one or two breaths, and then stop. A single bubble can be enough.

## Doing it with a grandparent

A grandparent can read quietly while the child colors, or draw at the other end of the table. A grandparent who lives far away can color the same picture while on a call, with the child holding up their page. For more ideas to try together, see the [together ideas](/together/) section.

## If it isn't working

- **The child refuses.** Accept it. Offer another idea at another time, or simply sit together.
- **The child becomes more upset.** Stop the activity, and give them space. Stay nearby and talk calmly. If this happens often, talk to your child's own clinician.
- **The child puts dough or crayons in their mouth.** Remove the item, and stop. Offer a safe object to hold instead.
- **The child wants to keep going.** Stop when you planned to stop, and say what happens next. Leave the materials for another time.

If you have a question about a child's health, ask your child's own clinician. These activities are not a substitute for that conversation.
