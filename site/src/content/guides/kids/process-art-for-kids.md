Process art is art where the doing is the point. A child mixes paint on a plate, presses a sponge into a puddle of color, or tears paper and layers the pieces. There is no picture to copy and no right answer, so the child decides what to try, how long to keep going and when to stop. The finished piece is a bonus, not the goal.

This guide is for parents, grandparents and carers of children from about 3 upward. It gives simple process art ideas and shows how an adult can take part and talk about the work without judging it.

## Who this suits

Process art suits children who like to experiment, who enjoy the feel of materials, or who would rather not be asked to draw a particular thing. It also suits children who find a blank page hard to start on, because there is no plan to get wrong. Children aged about 3 to 5 usually need an adult close by for paint, sponges and scissors. Older children can often work alone for a while, with an adult nearby.

These are suggestions, not diagnoses. Choose what your child enjoys.

## Safety first

- **Adult nearby.** Keep an adult close by for paint, glue, sponges and any tool.
- **Non-toxic, washable materials only.** Look for a clear non-toxic label. The Art and Creative Materials Institute uses an Approved Product seal on reviewed art materials. Read the label before the first use.
- **Small parts.** Under-3s should not have small pieces such as beads, buttons, bottle caps or small bits of tape. Choking hazards also include arts and crafts supplies, according to the Nemours KidsHealth choking guide. Keep materials out of the mouth.
- **No plastic bags.** Do not seal paint inside a plastic bag for a child to squeeze. Use a paper plate instead.
- **Scissors.** Use blunt scissors for children under about 6. An adult should cut any shape that needs a sharp blade.
- **Allergies.** Do not use latex balloons or latex gloves as materials. If the family avoids certain foods, check before using food, such as flour-based dough. Some families avoid food dyes, so use washable paint instead of food coloring.
- **Cleanup.** Put the cover on the paint, and keep a damp cloth close by.

## Simple process art ideas

### Mixing on a plate

**What you need:** a paper plate or a white dish; two or three washable paints in primary colors; a brush or spoon; a sheet of paper for the finished piece.

**Steps:**

1. Put a small blob of each color on the plate, a little apart.
2. Let the child push the colors together with a spoon or brush.
3. Ask, "What is happening?" and wait. Let the child decide.
4. When the child is ready, press a sheet of paper onto the plate and lift it off. Let the child choose whether to do this.

### Sponge stamping

**What you need:** a few clean sponges cut into shapes; washable paint in a shallow tray; a sheet of paper taped to the table.

**Steps:**

1. Show the child how to dip the sponge lightly in the paint and press it onto the paper.
2. Let them stamp in any order and place. Overlapping is fine.
3. Describe what you see: "The red and blue went over each other."
4. Let the child choose when the sheet is finished.

### Tearing and layering

**What you need:** colored paper or old magazine pages; a glue stick; a sheet of card.

**Steps:**

1. Tear the paper into strips and pieces. Let the child tear as much or as little as they like.
2. Glue one piece on the card. Let the child place the next one.
3. Ask, "Which one goes on top?" and let the answer be whatever they choose.
4. Stop when the child stops. Leave the piece to dry flat.

## How to comment without judging

Many adults want to say something kind about a child's art and reach for the same few words: "Good job," "Beautiful," "Is that a dog?" These can close the conversation. Some children may start to make work for the adult rather than for themselves, and a child asked "What is it?" may feel quizzed.

Try these instead.

- **Describe.** "I can see three colors on this sheet." "The lines go all the way to the edge."
- **Ask an open question.** "What did you try there?" "Where will you go next?"
- **Wait.** Give the child time to answer. Many children answer after a pause.
- **Use their words.** If the child says, "It's a storm," you can say, "The storm is on the left."
- **Name the effort.** "You worked on that for a long time."

If a child asks, "Do you like it?", you can answer honestly: "I like the blue. What do you like best about it?"

## Easier and calmer versions

- **Easier:** Use a bigger sheet and fewer colors. Pre-tear some paper for the child, or cut the sponge shapes in advance.
- **Easier to grip:** Use a chunky sponge with a thick handle or a jumbo brush. Tape the paper so it does not slide.
- **Calmer:** Work with one color and one tool. Play quiet music or no music at all.
- **Less mess:** Use a tray with raised sides, or put the work on a towel on the floor.
- **Fewer choices:** Lay out two colors instead of six. Offer choices one at a time.

## Doing it with a grandparent

A grandparent can make their own sheet alongside the child, using the same materials. Both sheets can be kept together. The grandparent may find it easier to stay quiet and watch, which many children enjoy. A grandparent who is not used to art may find the rule "no right answer" a relief. For more joint activities, see the [together ideas](/together/) section.

If a grandparent prefers to sit and watch, that counts. The child can show them each sheet as it is finished.

For another open-ended idea to try with an older child, [Paint a shared picture](/activity/paint-a-shared-picture/) takes two people and one sheet of paper.

## If it isn't working

- **The child wants to stop after a minute.** That is a good session. Thank them for trying, and end.
- **The child is upset that their piece does not look how they wanted.** Do not fix it for them. Ask whether they want to try again on a new sheet, or to make something different with the same materials.
- **The child copies your piece exactly.** Ask them to choose a different color or place. If copying is what they enjoy, that is fine too.
- **The table is too messy.** Shorten the session, or use a smaller area and fewer colors next time.
- **The child does not want to start.** Make a mark yourself on a sheet of paper and offer them the brush. Do not press the child to join in.

If you have a question about a child's health or development, ask your child's own clinician. This guide is about making art together, not about how a child is doing.
