Start with the person, not the list. The cards below work for older adults with or without memory changes, and each card explains its own steps and easier versions. This page covers how to pick among them, how to ask without pressure, and how to make it a habit.

## How do I match an activity to the person?

Look at what they have enjoyed for most of their lives, then at how they feel today. Both matter.

- **Past work and hobbies.** Someone who kept accounts may like sorting buttons by color. Someone who sang in a choir may prefer the songbook. Ask open questions, such as "What did you like making when you were younger?"
- **Present energy.** Many people have a better hour in the morning or after a meal. Choose a short, seated activity on a low-energy day and a longer one when they are rested.
- **Hearing.** Sit face to face, turn off the television, speak one step at a time, and keep music at a gentle volume. Check that hearing aids are in use before you start.
- **Eyesight.** Use daylight or a lamp placed to the side, large print and bold colors. Activities with big, simple shapes are often easier than ones with fine detail.

## How do I invite someone without pressure?

Make the first step small and shared. "I could use a hand with these cards" lands better than "Do you want to do a craft?" Let them see you start, then offer a role, such as choosing the colors, holding the paper or naming the flowers.

| If they say or do this | Try this instead |
| --- | --- |
| "I can't draw." | "I need your eye on this." Make the first mark yourself and pass it over. |
| They look tired or unsure. | Try again at a better hour, or offer one simple part, such as sorting pieces. |
| They turn away from the table. | Keep the materials out and let them watch. Watching counts. |
| They ask what it is for. | Explain briefly, then say what you think they might enjoy about it. |

Avoid words like "test", "finish" or "right answer".

## How often should we do it?

Doing the same thing on the same day each week turns it into a routine people look forward to, and routines are easier for families to keep. A Thursday visit with a short craft, or a Sunday phone call with a song, can become a regular part of the week.

After each visit, write a short note about what they liked and what they skipped. Next time, start from that note. Do not quiz anyone about the last session. Say something like "Last time you liked the blue paint, so I brought more."

- Keep sessions to 20 to 40 minutes.
- Stop while they still seem to be enjoying it.
- Repeat what worked, and drop the rest without fuss.

## Where should I start?

Choose two or three cards that fit the person today: one craft, one conversation and one music or nature activity. Try [make a personal songbook](/activity/make-a-personal-songbook/) if music matters to them, [write a letter together](/activity/write-a-letter-together/) if family is close by, or [press leaves and flowers](/activity/press-leaves-and-flowers/) after a walk outside.
