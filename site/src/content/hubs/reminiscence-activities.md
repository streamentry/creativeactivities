Reminiscence simply means sharing the past: looking at old photos, playing songs, cooking a family recipe or handling something kept for years. Done with care, it can be a warm and ordinary part of a visit. Done as a test, it can feel like an exam. Every card here is written to avoid that.

## How do you start without making it a quiz?

Start with the thing, not a question. Put a photo, a postcard or a recipe on the table and say what you can see. "That is a lovely blue dress." Leave room for them to join in, or not. If you ask anything, make it open and easy to pass on, such as "Do you like this one?"

- **Never ask for dates, names or places.** Offer them yourself if it helps.
- **Accept every answer.** A different name or year is not a mistake to fix.
- **Follow their lead.** If they want to talk about today instead, go with it.

## What if the past is painful?

Some people have memories they would rather not visit, and some photos bring back people who have died. Check the box or album first and set aside anything you think could upset them. If a memory upsets them during the activity, stop, stay with them and move to something lighter. The crisis route on each card lists who to contact if someone is in danger.

## Which activity suits which day?

On a quiet day, [looking through a photo album](/activity/look-through-a-photo-album/) or [sorting a box of postcards](/activity/sort-a-box-of-postcards/) needs only looking and choosing. With more energy, [a memory collage](/activity/make-a-memory-collage/), [a family recipe book](/activity/start-a-family-recipe-book/) or [a map of a favorite place](/activity/draw-a-map-of-a-favorite-place/) gives you something to keep. Music works on almost any day: [a personal songbook](/activity/make-a-personal-songbook/) or [a playlist](/activity/make-a-playlist-together/). The [reminiscence guide](/activities/reminiscence/) has more ideas.
