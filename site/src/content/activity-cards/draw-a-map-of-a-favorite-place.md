plainSteps:
  - Sit side by side with the paper between you.
  - Keep the pens within reach.
  - Ask whether there is a place they would like to draw.
  - It could be a street, a garden, a kitchen or a beach.
  - Make the first mark together, such as a line for a road.
  - Any mark will do.
  - Ask what comes next.
  - Draw it, or hand them the pen.
  - Any answer is the right one.
  - Add one thing at a time.
  - Let them talk or stay quiet as they choose.
  - Do not correct what they draw or say.
  - Do not ask for dates or names.
  - Stop when it feels finished, or when they have had enough.
  - Agree where to put the map.

safetyFlags: [none]
safety:
  - "A place can bring back strong feelings. Listen kindly and let them lead. Offer to put the pen down, or move on to something lighter."
  - "Never use the map to test them. Do not correct details or ask for dates, names or what happened."
  - "Keep the helper nearby and offer to draw. They do not need to be good at drawing, and neither do you."
  - "If a memory seems to upset them, stop drawing, sit with them and offer a drink or a change of scene."
stopNote: "It is fine to stop after one line. Put the map somewhere safe and carry on another day, or simply talk about the place for a while, or about something else."

A map is a way into a place, not a record of it. It does not need to be accurate, to scale or to look good. The drawing gives you both something to look at, and the talk follows only if and when they want it.
