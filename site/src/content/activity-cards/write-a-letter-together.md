description: "Write a short letter or postcard together to a grandchild or friend. The helper writes while you talk, with easier and bedside versions. Free and seated at a table."
summary: Write a short letter or postcard together, with the helper writing as the person says what to put.
enjoy: "People who like writing letters, keeping in touch with family or telling stories to a grandchild. Good for someone with plenty to say who would rather talk than write."
type: conversation
prepMinutes: 5
activityMinutes: [15, 30]
cost: free
mess: none
energy: low
supervision: nearby
settings: [tabletop, seated, bedside]
groups: [one-to-one, small-group, intergenerational]
materials:
  - "A sheet of notepaper or a plain postcard"
  - "A pen or pencil that is easy to grip"
  - "An envelope and stamp, if the letter will be posted"
  - "Optional: a pressed flower and a little tape, or a pencil for a small drawing"
substitutions:
  - "No notepaper? Use the back of an old greeting card or a sheet of printer paper."
  - "No stamp? Keep the letter to read aloud on a call, or give it in person."
senses: [sight, touch]
steps:
  - "Sit side by side at a table with the paper, the pen and the envelope in reach."
  - "Ask who the letter is for and what they would like to say. Any answer is the right one."
  - "Write the greeting at the top, in the words the person chooses."
  - "Ask one open question, such as what has been on their mind this week, and write down the answer."
  - "Read the sentences back aloud and ask if they want to change anything."
  - "Add a drawing, or tape a pressed flower to a corner."
  - "Sign the letter together, then fold it."
  - "Put the letter in the envelope, write the address and add a stamp, or set it aside to give in person."
variations:
  - kind: easier
    text: "Offer one or two ready-made openings to choose from, such as Thinking of you or Hope you are well."
  - kind: bedside
    text: "Use a clipboard on a lap tray, and keep the letter to one short paragraph."
  - kind: nonverbal
    text: "Offer two options for each line and write the one the person nods at or points to."
  - kind: low-vision
    text: "Use a dark felt-tip marker on large, plain paper, and read each line aloud as you write it."
  - kind: with-children
    text: "A child writes the greeting and adds a drawing while the older adult says what to put in the letter."
safety:
  - "Never write bank details, passwords or PINs in a letter. Check the address before sealing the envelope."
  - "Keep pens and stamps away from the mouth if the person may chew or taste things."
  - "Check before adding a flower; some plants can cause skin irritation or allergy."
stopNote: "It is fine to stop after one sentence. Put the letter aside, and finish it another day, or simply talk about the person it is for."

A letter does not need good handwriting or a good memory. The helper holds the pen, and the person decides what to say, which is often more than they expected. The finished letter is something to send, read aloud or keep.
